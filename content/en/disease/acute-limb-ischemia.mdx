Acute limb ischaemia is a sudden loss or reduction of arterial blood flow to an arm or a leg that threatens the survival of the limb and needs urgent diagnosis and treatment.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> It is called acute when the symptoms have lasted less than two weeks.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> This article is mainly about the legs. Chronic narrowing of the leg arteries is covered in the article on [peripheral artery disease of the legs](../peripheral-artery-disease/).

## Why it happens

<Patient>

The main causes are thrombosis and embolism, when a blood clot suddenly blocks an artery, as well as injury and spasm of the artery.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

In embolism, the clot forms somewhere else and is carried in the bloodstream.<Cite n={1} /> Most often it comes from a diseased heart, and the main cause is [atrial fibrillation](../atrial-fibrillation/).<Cite n={1} />

In thrombosis, the clot forms in the artery itself, where the wall is already damaged.<Cite n={1} /> Most often this happens in atherosclerosis: a plaque in the artery ruptures, a clot forms on it and blocks the artery completely.<Cite n={1} /> A clot can also form where a bypass or a stent was placed before.<Cite n={1} /> Atherosclerosis of the leg arteries is a common cause of acute ischaemia.<Cite n={3} />

Rare causes are thrombosis of an aneurysm (usually of the popliteal artery, behind the knee) and [aortic dissection](../aortic-dissection/).<Cite n={1} />

Why it is so dangerous: blood flow stops suddenly, and bypass vessels have no time to develop, so the tissues die quickly.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline names thrombosis, embolism, trauma and vascular spasm as the main causes of acute limb ischaemia (ALI); among non-traumatic causes, the most common is acute thrombosis at the site of a previous bypass or stent, or progression of atherosclerosis.<Cite n={1} />

According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, heart disease causes 90–96% of arterial emboli: coronary heart disease (CHD), [myocardial infarction](../myocardial-infarction/), atrial fibrillation (AF), atherosclerotic cardiomyopathies and heart defects.<Cite n={1} /> A rare cause is paradoxical embolism: a thrombus from the veins reaches the arteries through a defect in the septum of the heart.<Cite n={1} />

Risk factors for ALI in patients with chronic ischaemia of atherosclerotic origin (Russian Ministry of Health guideline): previous peripheral revascularisation, AF, a lower ankle-brachial index (ABI) and a history of major amputation.<Cite n={1} />

Pathogenesis: rapid occlusion of an artery, usually at a bifurcation, with the thrombus propagating in both directions and blocking the collaterals; in the setting of atherosclerosis the process may begin more slowly, but once the collaterals are blocked it progresses just as fast.<Cite n={1} /> According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, irreversible muscle necrosis develops after 4–6 hours in embolism or after 12–24 hours in thrombosis.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

The signs come on suddenly.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline describes them with the "6 P's" rule: pain, pulselessness (no pulse), pallor, poikilothermia (coldness), paraesthesia (numbness, tingling) and paralysis.<Cite n={1} /> In most cases pain is the first sign: sudden and severe, especially in embolism.<Cite n={1} /> The leg becomes pale, mottled or bluish and cold, and below the blockage there is no pulse.<Cite n={1} />

The MSD Manual lists five P's: severe pain, pallor, paraesthesia (or loss of sensation), poikilothermia (coldness) and pulselessness. Paralysis, which occurs in severe cases, is often counted as the "sixth P".<Cite n={4} /> The pain can be worse than the look of the leg would suggest.<Cite n={4} />

Loss of feeling and movement is a sign that the limb is in danger of being lost.<Cite n={1} /> The longer the symptoms last, the lower the chance of saving it.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

In the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, paraesthesia is a sign that nerve structures are involved, and paresis or paralysis is a sign of critical ischaemia and impending necrosis.<Cite n={1} /> MSD: ALI should be considered in at-risk patients (for example, those with peripheral artery disease) who have pain out of proportion to physical findings, especially when it is of sudden onset.<Cite n={4} /> Signs of severe ischaemia according to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline: subfascial oedema (the limb is in a critical state) and muscle and joint contracture (the limb is not viable).<Cite n={1} />

Clinical categories (Rutherford) in ESC and ACC/AHA: I, the limb is viable and not immediately threatened; IIa, marginally threatened, salvageable if promptly treated; IIb, immediately threatened, requiring immediate revascularisation if the limb is to be saved; III, irreversibly damaged.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> ACC/AHA: loss of the arterial Doppler signal means the limb is threatened, and absence of both arterial and venous signals means possible irreversible damage (category III).<Cite n={3} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline uses the classification of I. I. Zatevakhin et al. (2002).<Cite n={1} />

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A person with suspected acute ischaemia is urgently examined by a doctor who can judge whether the leg can be saved and who can restore blood flow.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> The doctor checks the colour of the skin, temperature, pulses, movement and feeling.<Cite n={1} /> The doctor listens to the blood flow in the arteries and veins with a handheld Doppler device.<Cite n={3} />

Guidelines differ on whether the blood vessels should be imaged before treatment. The Russian Ministry of Health guideline and the European (ESC) guideline advise such a test as long as it does not delay treatment; the Russian guideline prefers CT angiography and sets a time frame: within 90 minutes of arrival at hospital. The American (ACC/AHA) guideline notes that in most cases treatment is started without additional tests.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

Then doctors look for the cause, so that it does not lead to ischaemia again: an ECG and heart rhythm monitoring to look for atrial fibrillation, and an ultrasound scan of the heart.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Treatment is not delayed to search for the cause.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Emergency evaluation by an experienced clinician able to assess limb viability and perform revascularisation: ESC, I C; ACC/AHA, 1 (C-EO); Russian Ministry of Health guideline, level of evidence 5, grade of recommendation C.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> MSD: the occlusion can be roughly localised to the bifurcation just distal to the last palpable pulse.<Cite n={4} />

Imaging. Russian Ministry of Health guideline: diagnostic imaging provided it does not delay the start of treatment (level of evidence 5, grade of recommendation C); CT angiography within 90 minutes of admission to hospital or of diagnosis (level of evidence 1, grade of recommendation B); duplex ultrasound or contrast-enhanced MR angiography as an alternative where technically available (level of evidence 1, grade of recommendation B).<Cite n={1} /> ESC: in cases of neurological deficit, urgent revascularisation is recommended, and imaging to guide treatment, provided it does not delay it (I C).<Cite n={2} /> ACC/AHA: the initial evaluation can be performed without non-invasive imaging (1, C-LD); in patients with a complex history of revascularisation, imaging may be reasonable (2b, C-EO).<Cite n={3} />

Search for the cause. ESC: a comprehensive medical history and determination of the cause of thrombosis and/or embolisation (I C).<Cite n={2} /> ACC/AHA: testing for a cardiovascular cause of thromboembolism can be useful (2a, C-LD): ECG, rhythm monitoring to detect AF, echocardiography.<Cite n={3} /> Russian Ministry of Health guideline: after revascularisation for embolism, a search for the source of embolism to prevent recurrence (level of evidence 5, grade of recommendation C).<Cite n={1} /> It is important to distinguish ALI from neurological disorders and [deep vein thrombosis](../deep-vein-thrombosis/).<Cite n={1} />

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The main goal is to restore blood flow as quickly as possible to save the leg.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> If the hospital does not have the right specialists, the person is urgently transferred to a vascular centre.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

While preparations are made to restore blood flow, doctors give a medicine that reduces blood clotting, so that the clot does not grow, and relieve the pain.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

Blood flow can be restored in different ways: the clot is removed with a catheter or during surgery (embolectomy, thrombectomy), dissolved with a medicine given through a catheter directly into the artery (catheter-directed thrombolysis), or blood is routed around the blockage (bypass).<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> The doctor chooses the method: the choice depends on how severe the ischaemia is and how long it has lasted, where the blockage is and what caused it, other illnesses and what the hospital can offer.<Cite n={1} />

After blood flow is restored, the calf muscles can swell inside their tight sheaths (compartment syndrome). Doctors watch for this and, if needed, urgently cut the sheaths open (fasciotomy).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

If the tissues have already died, blood flow is not restored: breakdown products from the dead muscle can cause organ failure. In that case an amputation is done.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

After treatment it is important to prevent it from happening again. If the cause was embolism due to atrial fibrillation, long-term treatment with anticoagulants (medicines that reduce blood clotting) is needed.<Cite n={3} /> After thrombosis caused by atherosclerosis, the doctor decides which medicines are needed.<Cite n={1} /> People who smoke need to stop smoking, and after surgery regular follow-up with a vascular surgeon is needed.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Revascularisation. ACC/AHA: in a salvageable limb, revascularisation (endovascular or surgical, including catheter-directed thrombolysis) is indicated (1, A); surgical thromboembolectomy and catheter-directed thrombolysis are both effective.<Cite n={3} /> ESC: urgent revascularisation in cases of neurological deficit; in the absence of severe neurological deficit, within hours of initial imaging, with an individual decision (I C).<Cite n={2} /> Russian Ministry of Health guideline: emergency revascularisation when there is no blood flow and there is a neurological deficit, including loss of motor function; in embolism into intact arteries, catheter embolectomy under local anaesthesia is considered the method of choice.<Cite n={1} />

Anticoagulation before revascularisation. The strength of the recommendation differs: ACC/AHA, 1 (C-EO), immediately on diagnosis unless contraindicated; ESC, IIb C; Russian Ministry of Health guideline, level of evidence 4, grade of recommendation C.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> According to ACC/AHA, its goal is to limit the propagation of thrombus and distal embolisation.<Cite n={3} /> Analgesia as soon as possible: ESC, I C.<Cite n={2} />

Transfer to a vascular centre: Russian Ministry of Health guideline, emergency transfer from a non-specialist hospital (level of evidence 4, grade of recommendation C); ACC/AHA, should be strongly considered if the necessary expertise is not available locally.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

Non-viable limb. ACC/AHA: revascularisation of non-viable tissue should not be performed (3: Harm, C-EO), because reperfusion can result in multiorgan failure and cardiovascular collapse.<Cite n={3} /> Russian Ministry of Health guideline: with marked ischaemic contracture of the knee or ankle joint, refrain from revascularisation and consider amputation (level of evidence 2, grade of recommendation B).<Cite n={1} />

Compartment syndrome. ESC: monitoring after revascularisation and treatment with fasciotomy (I C); ACC/AHA, the same (1, C-EO); Russian Ministry of Health guideline, emergency fasciotomy of at least two, and if possible all four, compartments of the lower leg (level of evidence 5, grade of recommendation C).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

Secondary prevention. ACC/AHA: in AF, long-term oral anticoagulation is important to prevent recurrence.<Cite n={3} /> Russian Ministry of Health guideline: after revascularisation for arterial thrombosis, drug therapy to reduce cardiac complications and slow the progression of atherosclerosis (level of evidence 5, grade of recommendation C); stopping smoking (level of evidence 3, grade of recommendation B).<Cite n={1} />

Prognosis. According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, despite earlier revascularisation, the amputation rate reaches 10–15%, and compared with intermittent claudication the risk of death is 3.3 times higher and the risk of major amputation 14.2 times higher.<Cite n={1} /> According to MSD, despite treatment, about 11% of patients require amputation.<Cite n={4} />

## When to call an ambulance

Acute limb ischaemia is an emergency.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away if a leg or an arm suddenly becomes very painful, pale or bluish, cold, numb or tingling, or weak, or if you cannot move the foot or the toes.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> This applies especially to people with atrial fibrillation or atherosclerosis of the leg arteries.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Do not wait for it to pass: according to ACC/AHA, skeletal muscle tolerates a lack of blood for only about 4–6 hours.<Cite n={3} />

After an operation on the arteries, seek medical help urgently if severe pain comes back, if feeling or movement is impaired, if the leg turns cold, numb or pale, or if a pulse that could be felt before has disappeared.<Cite n={1} />

Emboli can also reach other arteries at the same time, for example the carotid artery.<Cite n={1} /> If there are signs of a stroke (sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, a drooping face), call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away. As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
