Peripheral artery disease of the legs is a chronic condition in which the arteries of the legs are narrowed or blocked. It can progress from discomfort and pain on exertion in the buttock, thigh or calf to tissue damage in the leg that does not heal.<Cite n={1} /> The main cause is atherosclerosis: plaques form in the artery wall and narrow the artery or block it completely.<Cite n={1} /> Atherosclerosis is a disease of all the arteries, so most people with disease of the leg arteries also have significant disease of the arteries of the heart or brain.<Cite n={[1, 6]} /> The arteries of the heart are covered in the article on [coronary heart disease](../coronary-heart-disease/).

## Why it happens

<Patient>

Most often the leg arteries become narrowed or blocked because of atherosclerosis. Less often the cause is a blood clot in an artery (thrombosis), inflammation of the arteries, compression of the popliteal artery or other rare diseases.<Cite n={1} />

The main risk factors are older age, smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure ([hypertension](../hypertension/)) and high cholesterol ([dyslipidaemia](../dyslipidemia/)).<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> Men are affected somewhat more often than women.<Cite n={1} />

Why it hurts when you walk: the working muscles do not get enough oxygen because the artery that supplies them is narrowed. After a short rest, when the muscle is relaxed, blood flow recovers and the pain goes away.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline names atherosclerosis obliterans as the main cause of intermittent claudication (IC); IC can also result from thrombosis of the limb arteries, aortoarteritis, cystic adventitial degeneration and popliteal artery entrapment syndrome.<Cite n={1} /> Atherosclerosis obliterans also dominates among the causes of critical limb ischaemia (CLI).<Cite n={2} />

According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, smoking increases the risk of lower extremity peripheral artery disease (PAD) 2–6-fold and the risk of IC 3–10-fold.<Cite n={1} /> Smoking and other forms of tobacco use are strong, dose-responsive risk factors.<Cite n={5} />

Prevalence: according to MSD, 2–6% worldwide, and 15–20% after the age of 80.<Cite n={6} />

Patients with PAD, even when asymptomatic, are at increased cardiovascular risk.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Narrowed leg arteries do not necessarily cause symptoms.<Cite n={1} /> According to MSD, more than 20% of people with this condition have no symptoms.<Cite n={6} />

The main symptom is intermittent claudication: pain or discomfort in the leg muscles, most often in the calf, less often in the buttock, thigh or foot. The pain comes on when walking and goes away after a few minutes of rest.<Cite n={1} /> Where exactly it hurts depends on the level at which the arteries are affected; the pain can be on one side or on both.<Cite n={1} /> In most people the symptoms stay stable for years, and only in some cases does the disease progress to critical ischaemia.<Cite n={1} />

Critical ischaemia is a severe form of the disease. The leg hurts at rest, often constantly, usually in the toes or the front of the foot. The pain gets worse when lying down and at night, and eases if the foot is lowered. The foot may be cold and numb.<Cite n={2} /> Wounds (ulcers) appear that do not heal, most often on the toes and heel, often after a minor injury.<Cite n={6} /> In diabetes with reduced feeling in the foot, a person may not notice a small wound from the edge of a toenail or from tight shoes.<Cite n={2} /> Critical ischaemia is usually not preceded by a long period of claudication.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Typical intermittent claudication is pain, aching, cramping or a tired feeling in the buttocks, thigh, calf or foot that occurs consistently during walking, does not start at rest, does not improve during walking and is usually relieved within about 10 minutes of rest.<Cite n={5} /> In femoropopliteal disease, claudication typically occurs in the calf, and pulses below the femoral artery are weak or absent.<Cite n={6} /> Aortoiliac disease may cause claudication in the buttocks, thighs or calves, hip pain and, in men, erectile dysfunction (Leriche syndrome).<Cite n={6} />

Similar symptoms occur in musculoskeletal and neurological disorders.<Cite n={1} /> Pain from spinal stenosis (pseudoclaudication) is relieved only by sitting, not just by stopping, and the foot pulses remain intact.<Cite n={6} />

Pokrovsky–Fontaine classification: IC corresponds to stage 2 chronic arterial insufficiency; the conventional cut-off between mild IC (stage 2A) and clinically significant IC is a walking distance of 200 m.<Cite n={1} /> CLI corresponds to stages 3 and 4: stage 3 is constant pain without tissue defects, stage 4 is trophic lesions.<Cite n={2} /> CLI is defined as impaired patency of the main arteries lasting more than 2 weeks, with rest pain and/or a trophic lesion.<Cite n={[2, 5]} /> In the vast majority of cases trophic changes are located below the ankle joint.<Cite n={2} /> When below heart level, the foot may look dusky red (dependent rubor).<Cite n={6} />

The prognosis varies. According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, the amputation rate in patients with IC over 10 years is less than 10%.<Cite n={1} /> In CLI, according to ACC/AHA, 1-year mortality was historically estimated at 25–35% and the 1-year amputation rate at up to 30%; in a recent randomised trial these rates were lower.<Cite n={5} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

The doctor asks about pain when walking and at rest, examines the legs and feet, looks for wounds and checks the pulses in the groin, behind the knee and on the foot.<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

The main simple test is the ankle-brachial index (ABI): the blood pressure at the ankle is compared with the blood pressure in the arm.<Cite n={[1, 6]} /> An ABI of 0.90 or lower confirms the disease.<Cite n={[3, 5]} /> If the ABI is normal at rest but the disease is still suspected, it can be measured after walking.<Cite n={[1, 6]} /> In diabetes and kidney disease the leg arteries can be stiff, and a normal or high ABI does not rule out the disease; in that case the blood pressure in a toe is measured.<Cite n={[3, 4]} />

To see the narrowings, an ultrasound scan of the arteries (duplex ultrasound) is done first. If surgery is planned, a CT or MR angiogram may be needed, and if these are not available, invasive angiography.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Examination shows weakened pulses at different levels, loss of hair on the distal parts of the limb, muscle atrophy and slow capillary refill.<Cite n={1} /> Pulses are assessed in the femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries.<Cite n={5} />

The ABI is the ratio of the highest ankle systolic pressure to the highest arm pressure.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> ESC recommends the ABI as the first-line non-invasive test for screening and diagnosis, using an ABI ≤0.90 as the diagnostic criterion (I B).<Cite n={3} /> ACC/AHA: an ABI ≤0.90 is abnormal, 0.91–0.99 borderline, 1.00–1.40 normal and >1.40 non-compressible.<Cite n={5} /> With non-compressible arteries or an ABI >1.40, ESC recommends additional methods: toe pressure (TP), the toe-brachial index (TBI) or Doppler waveform analysis (I B); in diabetes or renal failure, TP or TBI is measured even when the ABI is normal (I C).<Cite n={3} /> NICE: do not exclude the disease in people with diabetes based on a normal or raised ABI alone.<Cite n={4} />

ESC recommends duplex ultrasound (DUS) as the first-line imaging method to confirm lesions (I C), and CT and/or MR angiography as adjuvant imaging techniques for preparing revascularisation in symptomatic patients with aorto-iliac or multisegmental disease (I C).<Cite n={3} /> NICE offers duplex ultrasound as first-line imaging to everyone for whom revascularisation is being considered.<Cite n={4} /> For planning revascularisation, the Russian Ministry of Health guideline names non-invasive tests (duplex ultrasound, CT angiography, MR angiography) and, when these are not available, angiography.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

Treatment aims to reduce pain when walking and to lower the risk of heart attack, stroke and complications in the leg; the doctor chooses the approach.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

The most important thing is to stop smoking and using tobacco in any form completely: this lowers the risk of heart and blood vessel complications and of the disease getting worse.<Cite n={[1, 3, 5]} />

Walking exercise is the first-line treatment for intermittent claudication.<Cite n={1} /> It works best as a programme supervised by a specialist; the doctor chooses the programme.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> During walking, extra blood vessels form that carry blood around the narrowed segment.<Cite n={1} />

Medicines are prescribed by a doctor. They are needed to control cholesterol, blood pressure and blood glucose.<Cite n={1} /> Statins, blood pressure medicines and antiplatelet medicines (medicines that make platelets less likely to stick together) lower the risk of heart attack and stroke.<Cite n={[1, 6]} /> There are also medicines that may increase the distance a person can walk without pain; whether they are needed is for the doctor to decide.<Cite n={1} />

If claudication seriously limits daily life and walking exercise and medicines have not helped, the doctor may suggest an operation to restore blood flow (revascularisation).<Cite n={[1, 3, 5]} /> According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, minimally invasive procedures through a puncture in an artery are done more often: the narrowing is widened with a balloon (balloon angioplasty) or a stent is placed; sometimes open surgery is needed, a bypass that routes blood around the blocked artery.<Cite n={1} /> Guidelines from different countries differ on when to place a stent.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> A plaque can also be removed from the artery (endarterectomy).<Cite n={6} /> The method is chosen according to where the lesion is, what it looks like and the person's general health.<Cite n={3} /> After the operation, the medicines, not smoking and regular check-ups need to continue.<Cite n={1} />

In critical ischaemia, doctors try to restore blood flow as early as possible, and treatment is provided by a vascular team.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> If there are ulcers, pressure is taken off the foot so that the wound can heal; infection is treated with antibiotics.<Cite n={3} /> Amputation is a last resort: it is used only when all options for restoring blood flow have been considered.<Cite n={[4, 5, 6]} />

Look after your feet: check them every day for wounds and skin damage, and avoid injuries, burns and tight shoes.<Cite n={6} /> The doctor examines your feet at every visit.<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ESC recommends cessation and abstinence from smoking of any kind to reduce the risk of myocardial infarction, death and limb ischaemia (I A); ACC/AHA recommends advising patients to quit at every visit.<Cite n={[3, 5]} />

Exercise. Russian Ministry of Health guideline: walking exercise as a first-line treatment for IC, supervised by a doctor or on one's own (level of evidence 2, grade of recommendation A).<Cite n={1} /> ESC: supervised exercise training (SET) is recommended in symptomatic PAD (I A); a training frequency of at least 3 times a week, sessions of at least 30 minutes and a programme of at least 12 weeks should be considered (IIa B).<Cite n={3} /> NICE: a supervised exercise programme for everyone with intermittent claudication.<Cite n={4} /> ESC: training to moderate or severe claudication pain may be considered, but improvements are also achievable with milder pain or pain-free training (IIb B); lower-limb exercise training is not recommended in CLI with wounds (III C).<Cite n={3} /> NICE advises exercising to the point of maximal pain, and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline to near-maximal pain.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

Drug-based secondary prevention. Russian Ministry of Health guideline: HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins) for all patients with clinical manifestations of PAD (level of evidence 1, grade of recommendation B); ESC: lipid-lowering therapy (I A); ACC/AHA: high-intensity statin therapy.<Cite n={[1, 3, 5]} /> Antiplatelet therapy: Russian Ministry of Health guideline, in PAD with IC to reduce the risk of MI, stroke and death (level of evidence 1, grade of recommendation B); ACC/AHA, single antiplatelet therapy in symptomatic PAD; ESC does not recommend systematically treating patients with asymptomatic PAD without clinically relevant atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease with antiplatelet drugs (III B), and does not recommend oral anticoagulant monotherapy for PAD unless there is another indication (III A).<Cite n={[1, 3, 5]} /> In hypertension, ESC recommends a systolic blood pressure target of 120–129 mm Hg, if tolerated (I A), and says ACE inhibitors or ARBs should be considered as first-line drugs (IIa B).<Cite n={3} />

Guidelines differ on drugs to increase pain-free walking distance: a drug that the Russian Ministry of Health guideline suggests using is not recommended by NICE; NICE allows a different drug only under certain conditions.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> MSD stresses that the first of these is not a substitute for risk factor modification and exercise.<Cite n={6} />

Revascularisation in IC. Russian Ministry of Health guideline: consider surgical treatment in lifestyle-limiting IC when conservative therapy is ineffective or contraindicated (level of evidence 2, grade of recommendation B).<Cite n={1} /> ESC: may be considered when PAD-related quality of life is impaired after 3 months of optimal medical therapy and exercise (IIb B); not recommended solely to prevent progression to CLI (III B) or in asymptomatic PAD (III C).<Cite n={3} /> ACC/AHA considers revascularisation a reasonable option in functionally limiting claudication with an inadequate response to treatment, including structured exercise.<Cite n={5} /> ESC recommends choosing the approach (endovascular or open) according to lesion location and morphology and the patient's general condition (I C).<Cite n={3} /> The risk of restenosis and reocclusion after revascularisation calls for long-term follow-up; ESC recommends following up patients with PAD at least once a year (I C).<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

CLI. The main treatment is surgical.<Cite n={2} /> ESC: revascularisation as soon as possible (I B), early recognition and referral to the vascular team (I C), offloading of the foot with ulcers to allow wound healing (I C), treatment of infection with antibiotics (I C).<Cite n={3} /> NICE and ACC/AHA recommend that a multidisciplinary team assesses revascularisation options before amputation.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> According to MSD, the indications for amputation are uncontrolled infection, unrelenting rest pain and progressive gangrene; the Russian Ministry of Health guideline advises preserving as functional a segment of the limb as possible.<Cite n={[2, 6]} />

## When to call an ambulance

Acute limb ischaemia is a sudden loss or reduction of blood flow to a limb that threatens the limb and needs urgent diagnosis and treatment.<Cite n={[5, 7]} /> Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if a leg suddenly becomes very painful, pale, cold, numb or tingling, if the pulse in the foot has disappeared or if it has become hard to move the leg.<Cite n={[7, 8]} /> The pain of acute ischaemia can be worse than the look of the leg would suggest.<Cite n={8} /> If a clot has completely blocked the artery, it is important to start treatment within a few hours to save the function of the limb.<Cite n={7} />

Seek medical help urgently if you have sores or ulcers on the foot that do not heal or heal too slowly, if the leg is very painful or numb at rest, or if parts of the foot have turned black (gangrene): these may be signs of critical ischaemia.<Cite n={2} />

In people with atherosclerosis of the leg arteries, the arteries of the brain are often affected too.<Cite n={6} /> If there are signs of a stroke (sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, a drooping face), call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away. As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
