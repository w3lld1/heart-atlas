Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a life-threatening condition in which a blood clot that formed somewhere else blocks the pulmonary arteries.<Cite n={3} /> Most often the clot comes from the deep veins of a leg.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> PE and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) are two presentations of one disease, venous thromboembolism (VTE).<Cite n={2} /> Worldwide, VTE is the third most frequent acute cardiovascular syndrome after heart attack (myocardial infarction) and stroke.<Cite n={2} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

PE usually starts with a clot in the deep veins of a leg.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> Clots form when blood pools in the veins, when it clots more easily, or when a vessel wall is damaged.<Cite n={3} />

The risk rises most after major trauma, surgery, leg fractures, joint replacement and spinal cord injury.<Cite n={2} /> Cancer and birth control pills that contain oestrogen also raise the risk; pregnancy and sitting still for a long time on a car or plane journey are weaker risk factors.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Clot formation is explained by the Virchow triad of hypercoagulability, venous stasis and endothelial injury.<Cite n={3} /> Major trauma, surgery, lower-limb fractures and joint replacements, and spinal cord injury are strong provoking factors for VTE; cancer is a well-recognised predisposing factor; oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives are associated with an elevated VTE risk.<Cite n={2} /> The ESC lists pregnancy and immobility due to sitting (for example, prolonged car or air travel) as weak risk factors (odds ratio < 2).<Cite n={2} />

PE-induced vasoconstriction contributes to the initial increase in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), and the abrupt increase in PVR results in right ventricular (RV) dilation.<Cite n={2} /> Increased PVR raises RV afterload and impedes RV outflow.<Cite n={3} /> RV failure due to acute pressure overload is considered the primary cause of death in severe PE.<Cite n={2} /> Pulmonary capillary blood flow decreases, leading to dead-space ventilation and hypoxaemia.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

The symptoms of PE vary and are often nonspecific.<Cite n={3} /> The most common are shortness of breath, chest pain that gets worse when breathing, cough, coughing up blood, feeling faint and fainting.<Cite n={3} /> Sometimes there are no symptoms, and PE is found by chance during tests for something else.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

In most cases PE is suspected in a patient with dyspnoea, chest pain, presyncope or syncope, or haemoptysis.<Cite n={2} /> Syncope is associated with a higher prevalence of haemodynamic instability and RV dysfunction.<Cite n={2} /> The most common symptoms are dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, cough, haemoptysis, presyncope and syncope; PE may be asymptomatic and discovered incidentally.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

First the doctor judges how likely PE is, from experience or with special scoring rules.<Cite n={2} /> A D-dimer blood test is often done: a normal result makes PE unlikely, while a raised result does not confirm it on its own.<Cite n={2} />

The main test is a CT scan of the pulmonary arteries with contrast dye (CT pulmonary angiography).<Cite n={2} /> A lung scan (scintigraphy) and an ultrasound of the heart, which can show strain on the right ventricle, are also used.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Pre-test clinical probability is assessed by implicit (empirical) clinical judgement or by prediction rules such as the revised Geneva rule and the Wells rule.<Cite n={2} /> The negative predictive value of D-dimer testing is high, and a normal level renders acute PE or DVT unlikely; the positive predictive value of an elevated D-dimer is low, and the test is not useful for confirming PE.<Cite n={2} />

Multidetector CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the method of choice for imaging the pulmonary vasculature in suspected PE; the planar ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan is an established diagnostic test.<Cite n={2} /> Acute PE may lead to RV pressure overload and dysfunction, which can be detected by echocardiography.<Cite n={2} /> In a patient with suspected PE, a proximal DVT on compression ultrasonography is sufficient to warrant anticoagulant treatment without further testing; such patients still undergo assessment of PE severity and risk of early death.<Cite n={2} />

In the ESC guideline, high-risk PE is defined by haemodynamic instability: cardiac arrest, obstructive shock or persistent hypotension.<Cite n={2} /> The 2026 AHA/ACC guideline introduces Acute Pulmonary Embolism Clinical Categories to refine severity classification, prognosis and treatment decisions.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The basis of treatment is medicines that reduce blood clotting (anticoagulants).<Cite n={3} /> If PE is severe and the circulation is failing, doctors try to restore blood flow quickly, most often with medicines that dissolve the clot (thrombolysis). If thrombolysis cannot be used, the clot is removed through a catheter or with surgery; surgery is also used when thrombolysis has not worked.<Cite n={3} /> The doctor decides which treatment is needed.

</Patient>

<Student>

Anticoagulation is the mainstay of treating acute PE.<Cite n={3} /> Primary reperfusion treatment, usually thrombolysis, is the treatment of choice for haemodynamically unstable acute PE.<Cite n={3} /> Surgical pulmonary embolectomy and percutaneous catheter-directed therapy are alternative reperfusion options in patients with contraindications to thrombolysis; surgical embolectomy is indicated in unstable PE when thrombolysis (systemic or catheter-directed) is contraindicated or has failed.<Cite n={3} /> In the ESC guideline, unequivocal signs of RV pressure overload in a haemodynamically compromised patient with suspected PE justify emergency reperfusion if immediate CTPA is not feasible.<Cite n={2} />

Pulmonary embolism response teams (PERTs), made up of specialists in pulmonary medicine, critical care, cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery, have emerged as a new approach to managing PE.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

PE is life-threatening.<Cite n={3} /> The most severe forms present as cardiac arrest, shock or persistently low blood pressure.<Cite n={2} /> Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood, feeling faint or fainting appear.<Cite n={2} /> This matters especially for people who recently had surgery, major trauma or a leg fracture: these strongly raise the risk.<Cite n={2} /> As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
