Short QT syndrome (SQTS) is a rare inherited channelopathy: the QT interval on the [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) is shortened, and the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias is raised.<Cite n={1} /> The ion channel changes in it are the opposite of those in [long QT syndrome](../long-qt-syndrome/).<Cite n={3} /> Together with long QT syndrome, [Brugada syndrome](../brugada-syndrome/) and [catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia](../catecholaminergic-polymorphic-ventricular-tachycardia/), SQTS is one of the channelopathies: diseases in which arrhythmias arise without obvious structural heart disease.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> Its main danger is polymorphic [ventricular tachycardia](../ventricular-tachycardia/) and [ventricular fibrillation](../ventricular-fibrillation/), which often cause sudden death.<Cite n={3} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

The cause is changes (mutations) in genes for the ion channels of heart cells.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> For example, potassium channels that end the cell's activation work harder than usual, or sodium or calcium channels work more weakly than usual.<Cite n={3} /> As a result, the cells of the atria and ventricles recover faster after each activation, which sets the stage for dangerous arrhythmias in which the electrical activation travels in a circle.<Cite n={1} />

Most often the syndrome is inherited in an autosomal dominant way, meaning that one parent can pass it on to a child; the disease shows itself more often in men.<Cite n={3} /> Very rarely a short QT is acquired.<Cite n={3} /> The syndrome is very rare, but it probably often goes unrecognised.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Shortening of the action potential and effective refractory period of atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes sets the stage for life-threatening re-entrant arrhythmias.<Cite n={1} /> According to MSD, the action potential shortens most in the ventricular epicardium; the resulting transmural dispersion of repolarisation may lead to polymorphic VT or VF.<Cite n={3} /> The mechanisms are gain of function of repolarising potassium channels, or loss of function of depolarising sodium or calcium channels or of the Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger.<Cite n={3} />

Mutations are found most often in the potassium channel genes KCNH2 (IKr), KCNQ1 (IKs) and KCNJ2 (IK1) and in the anion exchanger gene SLC4A3 (AE3); in its section on SQTS the Russian Ministry of Health guideline also names the calcium channel genes CACNA1C and CACNB2b.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Together, documented channelopathies account for up to 40% of sudden cardiac deaths in non-athletes aged 35 or younger without structural heart or coronary artery disease; this is the share of the whole group, not of SQTS alone.<Cite n={5} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

As long as there is no arrhythmia, the person feels nothing.<Cite n={3} /> If [atrial fibrillation](../atrial-fibrillation/) or a ventricular arrhythmia starts, there may be palpitations, feeling faint, fainting or cardiac arrest.<Cite n={3} /> The syndrome is life-threatening at any age, including in babies in the first months of life.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, 40% of people with symptoms of SQTS have had a cardiac arrest by the age of 40.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A doctor will think of SQTS if a person without heart disease has had an unexplained cardiac arrest or fainting, or relatives have had this, and also if a short QT is found by chance on an ECG done for another reason.<Cite n={3} />

The main test is an ordinary 12-lead ECG.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> The diagnosis is not made on one number: along with the length of the QTc, the doctor considers past fainting and cardiac arrests, family history and genetic test results.<Cite n={3} /> QTc thresholds for the diagnosis differ between guidelines.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> If the QT is short but there are no other signs, the person may simply be monitored.<Cite n={3} /> Further tests may include longer heart rhythm recording, such as [Holter monitoring](../../diagnostic/holter-monitoring/), and tests that show the structure of the heart, such as [echocardiography](../../diagnostic/echocardiography/).<Cite n={3} />

Genetic testing is recommended once the diagnosis is made, but it finds a mutation in only a minority of patients, so a negative result does not rule out the syndrome.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> First-degree relatives are asked about symptoms and have an ECG; if a mutation has been found in the patient, they are offered a genetic test even if they have no complaints and their ECG is normal.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> If a relative died suddenly and the post-mortem found no clear cause, close relatives are advised to see a cardiologist.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ESC 2022: it is recommended that SQTS is diagnosed with a QTc ≤ 360 ms and at least one of a pathogenic mutation, a family history of SQTS, or survival from a VT/VF episode without heart disease (I C).<Cite n={2} /> SQTS should be considered with a QTc ≤ 320 ms (IIa C); with a QTc of 320–360 ms, it should be considered after arrhythmic syncope (IIa C) and may be considered if a relative died suddenly before the age of 40 (IIb C).<Cite n={2} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline makes the diagnosis in adults using the Gollob score (Appendix G2): points are given for the QTc (< 370 ms: 1 point, < 350 ms: 2, < 330 ms: 3) and also for other ECG features, arrhythmias and fainting, family history and genotype; with 4 points or more the probability of SQTS is high.<Cite n={1} /> In children and adolescents under 20, the refined criteria are a QTc < 316 ms, a corrected J–Tpeak < 181 ms and early ventricular repolarisation.<Cite n={1} /> NICE, when an ECG is read manually after a transient loss of consciousness in people over 16, counts a corrected QT of less than 350 ms as short; this is a threshold for the first assessment of a blackout, not for the diagnosis.<Cite n={4} />

Genetic testing is indicated in everyone diagnosed with SQTS (ESC, I C), but it finds a mutation in only 20% of patients according to the Russian guideline and in 20–30% according to MSD.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> An implantable loop recorder should be considered in young patients (IIa C).<Cite n={2} /> Programmed electrical stimulation is not recommended for SCD risk stratification in SQTS (ESC III C; Russian guideline: in adults).<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The main way to prevent sudden death is an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD); it is recommended for people who have survived a cardiac arrest or who have had sustained ventricular tachycardia recorded.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> How an ICD works is explained in the article on [ventricular fibrillation](../ventricular-fibrillation/). After a faint that was probably caused by an arrhythmia, an ICD is also discussed.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> In other cases the decision is made individually, taking into account family history and the length of the QTc: there are no general rules yet.<Cite n={1} />

If an ICD is needed but cannot be used or the person declines it, and also for a person without symptoms whose family has had sudden deaths, the doctor may consider an antiarrhythmic medicine.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> The guidelines differ on which one.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> The ESC advises that athletes with heart diseases that carry a risk of sudden death are managed according to separate guidelines on sports eligibility.<Cite n={2} /> The doctor decides on the approach together with the patient.

</Patient>

<Student>

An ICD is recommended in SQTS for survivors of cardiac arrest and/or patients with documented spontaneous sustained VT (ESC I C; Russian guideline: I C on the ESC scale).<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> An ICD should be considered after arrhythmic syncope (ESC IIa C).<Cite n={2} />

An antiarrhythmic drug may be considered when an ICD is indicated but contraindicated or refused, and in patients without symptoms with a family history of SCD (IIb C); the ESC and MSD name one drug for this, and the Russian guideline names a different one, and only for patients over 18.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

**Someone suddenly collapses, does not respond and is not breathing or is only gasping**: this may be cardiac arrest.<Cite n={6} />

- Shout for help and call your local emergency number at once (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK); if someone else is there, let them make the call and, if possible, fetch a defibrillator.<Cite n={6} />
- Start CPR without delay, as the ESC and the MSD Manual advise.<Cite n={[2, 6]} /> How to do chest compressions and use an automated external defibrillator is explained in the article on [ventricular fibrillation](../ventricular-fibrillation/).

If a person has lost consciousness, even briefly, call an ambulance too.<Cite n={1} /> Fainting during physical exertion is especially worrying: NICE treats it as a reason for urgent cardiovascular assessment.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline advises calling an ambulance if an episode of arrhythmia comes with dizziness, weakness, feeling faint, loss of consciousness or a racing heartbeat, and also for any episode of feeling faint or losing consciousness.<Cite n={1} /> An ambulance is also called when an implanted cardioverter-defibrillator has gone off.<Cite n={1} />

Even if the person has come round, NICE (for people over 16) treats the following as red flags after a blackout: an ECG abnormality, including a short QT interval, a blackout during exertion, and a family history of sudden cardiac death in people younger than 40 or an inherited heart condition: such a person is referred urgently for cardiovascular assessment, and a specialist reviews the referral within 24 hours.<Cite n={4} />
