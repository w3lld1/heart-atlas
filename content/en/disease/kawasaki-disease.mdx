Kawasaki disease, also called mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, is an acute inflammation of the artery walls (vasculitis) that occurs mainly in children under 5.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> It affects medium-sized arteries, most importantly the coronary arteries, which supply the heart.<Cite n={4} /> Without treatment, coronary artery dilation or aneurysms develop in about a quarter of children.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> In developed countries Kawasaki disease is the leading cause of acquired (not congenital) heart disease in children.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> How the coronary arteries are arranged is explained in the article on the [coronary circulation](../../anatomy/coronary-circulation/).

There is no separate Russian Ministry of Health guideline on Kawasaki disease, so the course of the disease, its treatment and outlook are described from the MSD Manual and the abstracts of the American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statements.

## Why it happens

<Patient>

The cause of Kawasaki disease is unknown.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> According to the MSD Manual, an infection probably triggers an abnormal immune response in children with an inherited predisposition.<Cite n={5} /> There is no known way to prevent it.<Cite n={4} />

Most children who get it are under 5.<Cite n={3} /> Boys are affected slightly more often than girls; in adolescents, adults and babies under 4 months the disease is rare.<Cite n={5} /> Kawasaki disease occurs worldwide, but it is particularly common in children of Japanese descent.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, the male:female ratio is about 1.5:1; 80% of patients are younger than 5 years, with a peak at 6 to 24 months.<Cite n={4} /> About 2% of patients have a recurrence, typically months to years later.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline on myocarditis in children suggests that Kawasaki disease with myocarditis, coronary arteritis and, less often, acute heart failure (usually in boys under 5) may be a variant of infectious myocarditis of unknown cause.<Cite n={6} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

The illness begins with a high temperature, usually above 38 °C, that lasts at least 5 days.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> Fever-lowering medicines do not bring it back to normal.<Cite n={5} /> Besides the fever, there may be:<Cite n={[1, 5]} />

- redness of the whites of both eyes without discharge;
- red, dry, cracked lips, a bright red "strawberry" tongue, redness inside the mouth and throat;
- swelling and redness of the hands and feet;
- a rash of varying appearance, mostly on the trunk;
- swollen lymph nodes in the neck.

These signs can come and go, so the doctor asks the parents about everything that has happened since the fever began, even if some of it has gone by the time of the visit.<Cite n={1} /> About 10 days after the illness starts, the skin on the fingers and toes begins to peel.<Cite n={5} /> The illness can last from 2 to 12 weeks or longer.<Cite n={5} />

Children under 1 year may have fewer signs besides the fever, but their risk of coronary artery damage is higher than in older children.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, the fever in the acute phase is unremitting and is accompanied by irritability, occasional lethargy or intermittent colicky abdominal pain.<Cite n={4} /> The NICE committee notes that fewer than 4 principal features are often present early in the illness, and in incomplete Kawasaki disease fewer than 4 are present throughout.<Cite n={1} />

Cardiac manifestations usually begin in the subacute phase, about 1 to 4 weeks after onset, as the rash and fever subside, but they can also appear in the acute phase.<Cite n={4} /> Early manifestations include acute [myocarditis](../myocarditis/) with [heart failure](../heart-failure/), arrhythmias, endocarditis and [pericarditis](../pericarditis/); coronary artery aneurysms may form later.<Cite n={4} /> Giant aneurysms are rare but carry the greatest risk of thrombosis, infarction and cardiac tamponade.<Cite n={4} />

Russian Ministry of Health guidelines list Kawasaki disease among the rare causes of coronary artery disease in adults and among the causes of chronic heart failure in children due to coronary vessel involvement.<Cite n={[7, 8]} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

There is no specific test for Kawasaki disease: the diagnosis is based on the signs of the illness.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> According to the MSD Manual, it is diagnosed when a temperature above 38 °C has lasted 5 days or more and at least 4 of the 5 signs listed above are present.<Cite n={5} /> Blood and urine tests, cultures and a chest X-ray help rule out illnesses with similar symptoms, such as measles and scarlet fever.<Cite n={5} />

When Kawasaki disease is suspected, an [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) and an [ultrasound of the heart (echocardiography)](../../diagnostic/echocardiography/) are done to look for coronary artery aneurysms, leaking heart valves, pericarditis or myocarditis.<Cite n={5} /> Changes may not appear straight away, so the tests are repeated; the doctor decides when.<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

NICE recommends that every child with a fever lasting 5 days or longer should be assessed for Kawasaki disease.<Cite n={1} /> The AHA stresses that prompt diagnosis is essential and that, when the classic clinical criteria are incomplete, an updated algorithm uses supplemental information.<Cite n={2} /> According to MSD, atypical Kawasaki disease should be considered, and testing started, when a child has had a fever of 38 °C or higher for at least 5 days plus at least 2 of the 5 criteria.<Cite n={4} />

Laboratory tests are not diagnostic but help exclude other disorders.<Cite n={4} /> A similar picture can be caused by scarlet fever, staphylococcal exfoliative syndromes, measles, drug reactions and systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis; multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children after SARS-CoV-2 is also similar.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

Treatment is started as early as possible: treatment within the first 10 days of the illness significantly reduces the risk of coronary artery damage.<Cite n={5} /> The mainstay of initial treatment is immune globulin given into a vein; in addition, the child is given aspirin.<Cite n={[2, 5]} /> The doctor decides which medicines are needed and for how long.

Do not give a child aspirin yourself: in children with flu or chickenpox it slightly increases the risk of a very rare but life-threatening disorder called Reye syndrome.<Cite n={5} /> That is why doctors recommend flu and chickenpox vaccination for children who need long-term aspirin.<Cite n={5} />

If the coronary arteries are affected, treatment that protects against blood clots is continued for a long time, and anticoagulants (medicines that prevent blood from clotting) may be added for large aneurysms.<Cite n={[2, 5]} /> Children with aneurysms need lifelong follow-up by a cardiologist.<Cite n={2} />

According to the MSD Manual, children whose coronary arteries are not affected recover completely after treatment; when they are affected, the outcome depends on how severe the damage is.<Cite n={5} /> About two-thirds of aneurysms go away within a year, large ones less often; but even then the risk of heart problems in adulthood may be increased.<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to the AHA, intravenous immune globulin is the mainstay of initial treatment, and additional primary therapy is discussed for selected patients; approximately 10% to 20% of patients do not respond to initial intravenous immune globulin, and recommendations for additional therapies are given.<Cite n={2} /> According to the 2024 AHA update, criteria defining North American patients at high risk of coronary artery aneurysms who may benefit from more intensive initial treatment have been published.<Cite n={3} />

According to MSD, children should be treated by, or in close consultation with, an experienced paediatric cardiologist, paediatric infectious disease specialist or paediatric rheumatologist.<Cite n={4} /> According to MSD, effective therapy lowers the incidence of coronary artery aneurysms to less than 5%, and coronary thrombosis may require fibrinolysis or percutaneous intervention.<Cite n={4} />

For evolving coronary artery abnormalities, the AHA calls for more frequent assessments and escalation of thromboprophylaxis.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

If a child's temperature has lasted 5 days or longer, do not put off seeing a doctor: the child should be assessed for Kawasaki disease.<Cite n={1} /> Prompt diagnosis and treatment can prevent long-term heart complications.<Cite n={1} /> It is especially important not to wait if the child is under 1 year old: they may have fewer signs but a higher risk of coronary artery damage.<Cite n={1} />

Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if a child with a fever has signs that NICE places in the high-risk group for serious illness:<Cite n={1} />

- the child is younger than 3 months and the temperature is 38 °C or higher;
- pale, mottled, ashen or blue skin, lips or tongue;
- the child does not respond to you, does not wake, or falls asleep again after being woken;
- a weak, high-pitched or continuous cry;
- very fast breathing, more than 60 breaths a minute;
- grunting, or noticeable (moderate or severe) chest indrawing when breathing in;
- a rash that does not fade when pressed;
- a bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness or a prolonged seizure.

After Kawasaki disease with coronary artery aneurysms, a blood clot can form in an aneurysm, and this leads to a heart attack.<Cite n={5} /> The signs of a heart attack in adults are described in the article on [coronary heart disease](../coronary-heart-disease/). As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
