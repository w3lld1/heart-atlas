Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is the formation of a thrombus, that is, a blood clot, in a deep vein; the clot can block the vein.<Cite n={1} /> Deep veins run within the muscles and along the bones, while superficial veins lie under the skin.<Cite n={5} /> DVT most often develops in the veins of the calf, thigh or pelvis, and less often in the veins of the arms.<Cite n={4} /> The most dangerous complication of DVT is [pulmonary embolism (PE)](../../disease/pulmonary-embolism/): part of the clot breaks off and is carried by the blood through the heart into the lungs.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> DVT is the main cause of PE; both conditions are forms of venous thromboembolism (VTE).<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

  <p slot="disease">The clot usually starts at the cusps of a venous valve and, without treatment, can grow up the vein.<Cite n={4} /> It obstructs the outflow of blood through the vein, and the leg may swell.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> A piece of the clot can break off and be carried by the blood through the heart into the lungs: this is PE.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /></p>
</Diagram>

## Why it happens

<Patient>

A clot forms in a vein when blood flows more slowly than usual, when the vein wall is damaged or when the blood clots more easily.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> Blood in the leg veins slows down if a person lies down for a long time: the calf muscles do not contract and do not squeeze the blood towards the heart.<Cite n={5} />

That is why DVT is more common after operations and injuries and during long periods of immobility, including long journeys.<Cite n={1} /> In healthy people, thrombosis after sitting for a long time in a car or a plane is rare, and usually happens when there are other risk factors.<Cite n={5} /> The risk is also raised by cancer, an inherited tendency to thrombosis (thrombophilia), pregnancy and birth control pills.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> DVT becomes more common with age.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Thrombosis is classically explained by Virchow's triad: impaired blood flow (stasis), endothelial injury and hypercoagulability.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> DVT usually begins in the pockets of venous valve cusps; without treatment, the thrombus may propagate proximally.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline lists as major provoking factors surgery under general anaesthesia lasting more than 30 minutes, hospital bed rest for 3 days or longer because of an acute illness or an exacerbation of a chronic one, trauma with fractures, and caesarean section.<Cite n={1} />

By location, DVT is divided into proximal (thrombus in the popliteal, femoral or iliac veins or the inferior vena cava) and distal, that is, thrombosis of the deep calf veins without extension into the popliteal vein.<Cite n={1} /> Distal DVT is less likely to be a source of large emboli but can propagate to the proximal thigh veins and from there cause PE.<Cite n={4} />

According to the ESC, venous thromboembolism ranks third worldwide in prevalence, behind only myocardial infarction and stroke.<Cite n={3} /> Estimates of DVT incidence differ. The ESC gives an annual DVT incidence of 53 to 162 per 100,000 population.<Cite n={3} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline gives an incidence of isolated DVT in Europe of 45–117 per 100,000 people per year; the incidence of DVT and PE rises with age, in men and women alike.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Thrombosis can show itself as swelling of the calf or the whole leg and a bursting pain in the leg or in the calf muscles.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> The leg may be tender to the touch and warm, the skin may turn bluish, and the veins under the skin may become more visible.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> The longer the clot, the stronger the symptoms.<Cite n={1} />

But many people with DVT have no symptoms at all.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> Sometimes the first sign of thrombosis is PE: chest pain or shortness of breath.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> Swelling and pain in the leg also occur in other conditions, so thrombosis cannot be recognised from the symptoms alone.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Most symptoms of DVT are non-specific.<Cite n={1} /> The most severe course is seen when the iliac veins and the iliofemoral segment are involved; distal thrombosis may be asymptomatic or cause only calf pain.<Cite n={1} />

A similar picture can be caused by soft-tissue injury, cellulitis, a ruptured popliteal cyst (pseudo-DVT), or partial or complete tears of the calf muscles or tendons.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

A long-term consequence of DVT is post-thrombotic disease: the vein wall changes irreversibly, outflow of blood from the leg is impaired, and persistent swelling, pain, heaviness and skin colour changes appear, with venous (trophic) ulcers forming in the late stages.<Cite n={1} /> For more about it, see the article on [post-thrombotic syndrome](../post-thrombotic-syndrome/), another name for this condition. A rare, severe complication is phlegmasia cerulea dolens: near-complete blockage of the leg veins with massive swelling, bluish discolouration and severe pain; without prompt treatment, gangrene can develop.<Cite n={[1, 4, 5]} />

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

The doctor asks questions, examines the leg and assesses the probability of thrombosis with a special score, the Wells score.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> The score on its own neither makes nor rules out the diagnosis.<Cite n={1} /> If the probability is not high, a blood test for D-dimer is done: a substance released from blood clots.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> A normal D-dimer makes it possible to rule out DVT with high probability, while a raised D-dimer does not prove thrombosis, and then an ultrasound scan is needed.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

The main test is an ultrasound scan of the veins (duplex scanning): it shows the clot itself, impaired compressibility of the vein and impaired blood flow in it.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline and NICE recommend assessing the clinical probability of DVT with the Wells score; a score of 2 points or more means DVT is likely.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> When DVT is likely, NICE recommends a proximal leg vein ultrasound scan, with the result available within 4 hours if possible, and a D-dimer test if the scan is negative.<Cite n={2} /> If the scan is negative and the D-dimer is positive, NICE recommends a repeat ultrasound scan 6 to 8 days later.<Cite n={2} />

According to the ESC, compression ultrasonography of the veins has a sensitivity of more than 90% and a specificity of about 95% for proximal symptomatic DVT.<Cite n={3} />

Asymptomatic PE in DVT is common, but estimates vary. According to MSD, approximately 50% of patients with DVT have occult PE.<Cite n={4} /> According to data cited by the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, the rate of asymptomatic PE ranges from 13% in distal to 40–70% in proximal DVT.<Cite n={1} /> According to the ESC, compression ultrasonography shows DVT in 30–50% of patients with PE.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The goals of treatment are to stop the clot from growing, to let it dissolve and to lower the risk of complications: PE and post-thrombotic disease.<Cite n={1} /> Treatment is based on anticoagulants, medicines that reduce blood clotting.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> They do not dissolve a clot that has already formed, but they stop it from growing and help prevent new ones from forming.<Cite n={5} /> There are several groups of anticoagulants, given as injections and as tablets.<Cite n={[1, 5]} />

According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, anticoagulants are taken for at least 3 months, and the exact duration is decided individually, taking into account the thrombosis and the risk of bleeding.<Cite n={1} /> NICE also recommends treatment for at least 3 months for confirmed proximal DVT.<Cite n={2} /> Sometimes treatment is continued much longer.<Cite n={5} /> The most common complication of anticoagulants is bleeding, so only a doctor chooses the medicine and the duration.<Cite n={5} />

Many people are treated at home; they are admitted to hospital if the risk of complications is high or if surgery is needed.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> According to the MSD Manual, bed rest is needed only to relieve symptoms, and physical activity does not raise the risk of the clot breaking off.<Cite n={5} />

Sources differ on compression stockings. The Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommends elastic compression as early as possible after the diagnosis is confirmed, to lower the risk of post-thrombotic disease.<Cite n={1} /> NICE recommends not offering elastic graduated compression stockings after DVT to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome or recurrent venous thromboembolism; this recommendation does not cover stockings used to relieve symptoms in the leg.<Cite n={2} /> Whether stockings are needed is decided by the doctor.

</Patient>

<Student>

According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, the basic methods of treating DVT are anticoagulant therapy and elastic compression.<Cite n={1} /> Several groups of anticoagulants are used: injectable heparins (unfractionated or low-molecular-weight) and oral agents, including direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> The choice of drug, the dose and the duration are decided by the doctor.

The risk of recurrence is lowest with transient risk factors (surgery, trauma, temporary immobility) and highest with persistent risk factors (for example, cancer), idiopathic DVT or incomplete resolution of the thrombus.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline allows early surgical thrombus removal in selected patients with extensive DVT, to relieve symptoms quickly and lower the risk of post-thrombotic disease; it does not replace standard anticoagulant therapy.<Cite n={1} /> For symptomatic iliofemoral DVT, NICE suggests considering catheter-directed thrombolytic therapy only if all of the following apply: symptoms of less than 14 days' duration, good functional status, a life expectancy of 1 year or more and a low risk of bleeding.<Cite n={2} /> Thrombolytics increase the risk of bleeding.<Cite n={5} /> NICE suggests considering an inferior vena cava filter for proximal DVT or PE when anticoagulation is contraindicated, and recommends removing it once anticoagulation is no longer contraindicated and has been established.<Cite n={2} /> Unlike anticoagulants, a filter does not prevent new clots from forming.<Cite n={5} />

With timely, adequate treatment, the prognosis is generally good.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if you develop shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood, feeling faint or fainting: these may be signs of PE.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> In the acute phase of thrombosis, the risk of part of the clot breaking off is especially high.<Cite n={1} /> For more about PE, see the article on [pulmonary embolism](../../disease/pulmonary-embolism/). An ambulance is also needed if the leg has become very swollen, bluish or pale and is very painful: this is how a rare, severe form of thrombosis shows itself, in which gangrene can develop without prompt treatment.<Cite n={[1, 4, 5]} /> Swelling and pain in the leg without these signs are a reason not to put off seeing a doctor: timely diagnosis helps prevent complications.<Cite n={1} /> For comparison, the British NICE guideline recommends that when DVT is likely, a vein ultrasound scan is done with the result available within 4 hours if possible.<Cite n={2} /> As everywhere on the site: call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath; this is the general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
