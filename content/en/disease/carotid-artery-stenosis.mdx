Carotid artery stenosis is a narrowing of a carotid artery in the neck. This article is about narrowing caused by atherosclerosis, in which fatty deposits (plaques) build up in the artery wall.<Cite n={[5, 7]} /> The internal carotid arteries carry blood from the heart along the front of the neck to the brain.<Cite n={7} /> Narrowing of a carotid artery is a risk factor for ischaemic stroke that can be changed.<Cite n={7} /> Guidelines distinguish symptomatic stenosis, which has already caused a transient ischaemic attack (TIA), brief loss of vision in one eye or a stroke, from asymptomatic stenosis, and treat them differently.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

In atherosclerosis, fatty deposits (plaques) build up in the artery wall and narrow or block the artery.<Cite n={7} /> In the carotid artery they often grow where it divides in the neck.<Cite n={5} /> Risk factors for stroke that can be changed include high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, drinking too much alcohol, high cholesterol and obesity; many of them are also risk factors for atherosclerosis.<Cite n={[5, 7]} /> More about them in the articles on [hypertension](../hypertension/) and [dyslipidaemia](../dyslipidemia/).

The main danger of the narrowing is ischaemic stroke.<Cite n={7} /> A fragment of clot or plaque may break off and block an artery in the brain, or the brain beyond a severe narrowing may not get enough blood; sometimes both happen.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline names atherosclerosis of other arteries, those of the heart and legs, as one of the clues that a stroke was caused by atherosclerosis of a large artery, and the European guideline advises looking at the whole arterial system in people with arterial disease.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> See the articles on [coronary heart disease](../coronary-heart-disease/) and [peripheral artery disease](../peripheral-artery-disease/). The neck arteries can also narrow for other reasons, for example a tear in the artery wall (dissection), fibromuscular dysplasia or [Takayasu arteritis](../takayasu-arteritis/).<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Atheromas are common at areas of turbulent flow, particularly at the carotid bifurcation; ulcerated atheromas predispose to thrombi.<Cite n={5} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline names the main mechanisms of stroke as artery-to-artery embolism (by a thrombus or a fragment of atheroma), hypoperfusion or a combination of the two.<Cite n={2} /> Branches of the internal carotid artery form the anterior (carotid) circulation of the brain; the ophthalmic artery is one of its branches.<Cite n={6} />

According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, atherothrombotic stroke is a stroke in the territory of a large extracranial or intracranial artery with more than 50% stenosis or occlusion on angiographic imaging; with stenosis below 50%, an atherothrombotic cause may be supported by a highly embologenic plaque.<Cite n={2} /> In ICD-10, carotid stenosis is coded I65.2, "Occlusion and stenosis of carotid artery".<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Stenosis that has not caused a TIA or a stroke is called asymptomatic; it is found during tests, for example when people with several risk factors are screened.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> The narrowing can show itself as a TIA: a sudden episode of symptoms that usually lasts a few minutes and then goes away completely; most TIAs are caused by emboli, usually from the carotid or vertebral arteries.<Cite n={4} /> A TIA is often an early warning of a coming stroke: the risk of stroke is highest in the first day or two after it.<Cite n={[4, 7]} />

A typical sign of carotid narrowing is sudden, painless loss of vision in one eye that comes back, usually in less than 5 minutes.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> Other signs appear on the side of the body opposite the affected artery: weakness or numbness of the arm and leg, or loss of half of the field of vision. If the half of the brain responsible for language is affected, speaking, writing or reading are disturbed.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline defines a TIA as a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal ischaemia of the brain without an infarct.<Cite n={2} /> MSD describes a TIA as sudden transient neurological dysfunction, typically lasting less than 1 hour, without evidence of acute infarction on diffusion-weighted MRI.<Cite n={6} /> Retinal TIA (amaurosis fugax, transient monocular blindness) is transient, painless monocular blindness without residual visual impairment; it occurs when the ophthalmic artery is affected.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

For the carotid territory, the Russian Ministry of Health guideline lists contralateral hemiparesis, hemihypaesthesia and hemianopia, acute painless monocular loss of vision and, when the dominant hemisphere is affected, motor and/or sensory aphasia, agraphia, alexia and apraxia.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

After a TIA or a stroke, doctors check blood flow in the arteries of the neck and head with ultrasound and/or CT or MR angiography.<Cite n={[2, 4, 5]} /> The first test for finding a narrowing of the internal carotid artery is duplex ultrasound.<Cite n={1} /> If a person who has had a TIA may be a candidate for surgery, the arteries are imaged urgently.<Cite n={3} />

The narrowing is expressed as a percentage. There are two ways of calculating it, NASCET and ECST, which give different figures for the same narrowing, so the report must state which method was used.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> What the figures mean for treatment is for the doctor to decide.

</Patient>

<Student>

ESC (I C): duplex ultrasound is the first-line imaging test to diagnose ICA stenosis; the NASCET method or its non-invasive equivalent is recommended (I B), and the ECST method is not recommended (III C).<Cite n={1} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline also gives priority to NASCET: 50% stenosis by NASCET corresponds to 75% by ECST, and 60% to 80%.<Cite n={2} /> By peak systolic velocity criteria, ESC grades 50–69% stenosis as moderate and 70% or more as severe.<Cite n={1} /> MRA and CTA show the posterior (vertebrobasilar) circulation better than ultrasound.<Cite n={5} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommends assessing blood flow in the vessels of the neck and head by radiological and/or ultrasound methods after a TIA, and, after a stroke in candidates for intervention, duplex ultrasound of the carotid arteries within 24 hours of admission.<Cite n={2} /> ESC allows screening for asymptomatic stenosis (IIb C) in people with two or more cardiovascular risk factors.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The basis of treatment is control of risk factors and medicines that lower cholesterol and blood pressure, as well as medicines against blood clots (antiplatelet medicines); in asymptomatic stenosis these are prescribed taking the risk of bleeding into account.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> Stopping smoking in any form, a healthy Mediterranean-style diet and physical activity are important.<Cite n={1} /> The doctor chooses the medicines and doses.

The plaque can be removed by an operation, carotid endarterectomy.<Cite n={7} /> Another option is stenting: the narrowing is widened from inside the artery and a stent is left in it.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian guideline puts the operation first and considers stenting an alternative, for example when the anatomy is unfavourable for surgery or open surgery carries a high risk.<Cite n={2} /> The decision is made by several specialists together; for symptomatic stenosis, the European guideline advises that the team include a neurologist.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

If the narrowing has already caused a TIA or a minor stroke and an operation is indicated, it is done early, within the first two weeks.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> A mild narrowing is not operated on.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> In asymptomatic stenosis, a planned operation is not done for everyone: it is discussed when there are signs of a higher risk of stroke.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> During follow-up, the doctor checks symptoms, risk factors and whether medicines are being taken, and after an operation or stenting checks the artery with ultrasound.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Optimal medical therapy is recommended for all patients with symptomatic ICA stenosis (ESC, I A); the Russian Ministry of Health guideline includes antiplatelet, high-intensity lipid-lowering and antihypertensive therapy, and NICE best medical treatment includes control of blood pressure, antiplatelet agents, cholesterol lowering and lifestyle advice.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> In asymptomatic stenosis of more than 50%, ESC advises considering long-term antiplatelet therapy if bleeding risk is low (IIa C).<Cite n={1} /> Drugs and doses are not given here.

Symptomatic stenosis: CEA is recommended for 70–99% stenosis (ESC I A, Russian Ministry of Health) and should be considered for 50–69% stenosis (ESC IIa A), provided the documented 30-day risk of procedural death or stroke is below 6%; the Russian Ministry of Health guideline also recommends CEA for 50–69% stenosis; NICE refers people with 50–99% stenosis by NASCET urgently for CEA.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> If indicated, ESC recommends CEA within 14 days (I B), and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline within 14 days of the ischaemic event unless early surgery is contraindicated.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> Revascularisation is not recommended for stenosis below 50%.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> ESC advises considering stenting in symptomatic patients at high risk for CEA with 70–99% stenosis (IIa B).<Cite n={1} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommends CEA as the priority and regards carotid stenting as an alternative when anatomical or medical conditions raise the risk of CEA.<Cite n={2} />

Asymptomatic stenosis: in the absence of high-risk features and with a life expectancy below 5 years, routine revascularisation is not recommended (ESC III A).<Cite n={1} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommends surgery for 60–99% stenosis by NASCET with at least one feature of increased risk, for example silent ischaemic lesions in the ipsilateral hemisphere, intraplaque haemorrhage or progression of the stenosis by more than 20%, provided life expectancy is at least 5 years.<Cite n={2} /> ESC lists the same features as high risk: ipsilateral silent infarction, stenosis progression of more than 20% and intraplaque haemorrhage.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

The main danger of stenosis is TIA and stroke, which are emergencies. Call an ambulance straight away on your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if there is sudden weakness or numbness of one side of the face, an arm or a leg, difficulty speaking, sudden confusion or difficulty understanding speech, loss, dimming or blurring of vision, particularly in one eye, or sudden dizziness or loss of balance, even if the symptom goes away quickly.<Cite n={7} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline describes a quick test to recognise a stroke: ask the person to smile, to speak and to raise both arms. If they suddenly cannot do one of these, call an ambulance straight away, describe the symptoms and tell the paramedics when they began. Do not give the person anything by mouth: stroke is treated only in hospital.<Cite n={2} /> If the symptoms went away within a few minutes, it may have been a TIA. Still call an ambulance: the risk of stroke is highest in the first day or two, and a specialist should see the person within 24 hours of the start of symptoms.<Cite n={[3, 4]} />
