Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is a congenital condition in which the <Structure id="aortic-valve">aortic valve</Structure> has only two cusps instead of the normal three.<Cite n={[7, 8]} /> It is the most common congenital cardiovascular abnormality.<Cite n={7} /> According to the American guideline on aortic disease (ACC/AHA), BAV affects approximately 1% of the population and is 2 to 3 times more common in men than in women.<Cite n={1} />

Such a valve can work normally for a long time.<Cite n={8} /> But it may become narrowed ([aortic stenosis](../aortic-stenosis/)) or fail to close properly ([aortic regurgitation](../aortic-regurgitation/)), it can be affected by [infective endocarditis](../infective-endocarditis/), and many people with BAV develop widening of the <Structure id="aorta">aorta</Structure>.<Cite n={[1, 7]} /> That is why it matters to know about BAV even when the valve works normally: the person needs follow-up.<Cite n={7} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

BAV is a congenital valve condition: a person is born with it.<Cite n={1} /> While the valve develops, two of its three cusps usually fuse, so instead of three cusps there are two: one larger and one smaller.<Cite n={4} />

Most often BAV appears in a person with no one else in the family affected, but it can be inherited.<Cite n={[1, 8]} /> BAV may be the only finding, or it may come with other congenital defects of the heart and vessels, such as [coarctation of the aorta](../coarctation-of-the-aorta/), or with diseases of the aorta.<Cite n={[1, 7]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Morphologically, BAV is usually fusion of two of the three aortic valve leaflets, seen on echocardiography; fusion of the right coronary and left coronary leaflets is the most common morphology.<Cite n={7} /> Children with fusion of the right coronary and noncoronary leaflets are more likely to have progression of valve dysfunction and to require intervention during childhood.<Cite n={7} /> BAV can lead to either aortic stenosis or regurgitation, depending on the degree of commissural fusion.<Cite n={4} /> According to the Russian Ministry of Health guidelines, a bicuspid valve is a not uncommon congenital cause of aortic regurgitation, and the most common cause of aortic stenosis in adults is calcification of the leaflets of a normal tricuspid or a congenital bicuspid valve.<Cite n={[5, 6]} />

BAV most often occurs sporadically but may be inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with variable penetrance; its prevalence in the relatives of a patient with BAV ranges from 9% to 20%.<Cite n={1} /> A coexisting BAV is present in about 50% of patients with coarctation of the aorta and in about 10% of patients with Loeys–Dietz syndrome.<Cite n={1} /> In Turner syndrome, 12% to 38% of patients have a bicuspid valve.<Cite n={4} />

Patients with BAV often have dilation of the aortic root, the ascending aorta or both, and the prevalence of aortic aneurysm increases with age.<Cite n={1} /> The ascending aorta is most commonly involved; the reported prevalence of aortic dilation ranges from 20% to 84%, depending on the population studied and the definition used.<Cite n={1} /> Dilation of the aortic root (the "root phenotype") accounts for 10% to 20% of patients with BAV and aortopathy; it may bring more rapid aortic growth and a higher risk of aortic complications.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

While the valve works normally, BAV may cause no problems in childhood and adolescence, and problems may appear in adulthood.<Cite n={[7, 8]} /> Symptoms depend on which complication develops.<Cite n={[7, 8]} /> If the valve becomes narrowed or leaks, the person may tire easily and may have shortness of breath, fainting, awareness of the heartbeat (palpitations) and chest pain.<Cite n={8} /> Infective endocarditis may cause a fever.<Cite n={[7, 8]} />

In many cases a widened aorta causes no symptoms at all, so it is looked for during check-ups.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Examination reveals an early systolic click and a systolic ejection murmur and, if aortic regurgitation is present, a diastolic decrescendo murmur.<Cite n={7} /> The click is often best heard with the patient sitting: at the apex, just after the first heart sound (S1), simulating a prominent split of S1.<Cite n={7} />

Patients with BAV without significant aortic dilation are at low risk of type A aortic dissection, whereas those with aneurysmal dilation of the aortic root, ascending aorta or both have a significantly higher risk.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> ACC/AHA and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline list as risk factors for dissection in BAV a family history of aortic dissection, an aortic growth rate of 0.3 cm per year or more, aortic coarctation and root phenotype aortopathy.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A doctor may suspect BAV on hearing a heart murmur or a clicking sound through the stethoscope; the diagnosis is confirmed by a heart ultrasound ([echocardiography](../../diagnostic/echocardiography/)).<Cite n={8} /> This test shows the structure and function of the valve and the size of the aortic root and ascending aorta, and helps to find coarctation of the aorta and other defects.<Cite n={1} /> If the aorta cannot be seen fully on ultrasound, a CT or MRI scan is done.<Cite n={1} />

People with BAV need lifelong monitoring of the aorta, even after valve replacement; the doctor decides how often to repeat the scans based on the size of the aorta and how fast it grows.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

Close relatives (parents, brothers and sisters, children) are advised to have a heart ultrasound, especially if the person with BAV has a widened aorta.<Cite n={[1, 4, 8]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ACC/AHA: in patients with BAV, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is indicated to evaluate valve morphology and function, the diameter of the aortic root and ascending aorta, and to evaluate for aortic coarctation and other associated cardiovascular defects (class 1); CT or MRI of the thoracic aorta is indicated when the aortic root and ascending aorta cannot be assessed accurately or completely by TTE (class 1).<Cite n={1} /> ESC recommends an initial TTE to assess the diameters of the aorta at several levels when BAV is first diagnosed (I B), and CT or MRI of the entire thoracic aorta at first diagnosis and when important discrepancies in measurements are found between subsequent TTE controls (I C).<Cite n={2} />

The link also works the other way: unexplained dilation of the aortic root or ascending aorta should prompt suspicion of an underlying BAV, and when a thoracic aortic aneurysm is identified, ESC recommends assessment of the aortic valve, especially for BAV (I C).<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

ACC/AHA recommend screening of all first-degree relatives by TTE in patients with BAV and a dilated aortic root or ascending aorta (class 1) and consider it reasonable for relatives of all patients with BAV (class 2a); ESC recommends it for relatives of BAV patients with root phenotype aortopathy and/or isolated aortic regurgitation (I C) and says it should be considered for relatives of all BAV patients (IIa B).<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> A medical genetics evaluation is recommended in patients with BAV and either heritable thoracic aortic disease or features concerning for Loeys–Dietz syndrome; most patients with BAV and thoracic aortic aneurysms who undergo genetic testing are not found to have a pathogenic variant, even when the condition is familial.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

There is currently no generally accepted medicine that changes the course of BAV or slows its progression.<Cite n={4} /> An intervention on the valve is considered when the valve starts to work very poorly. Usually symptoms have appeared by then, but sometimes surgery is recommended without them, for example for severe narrowing of the valve with reduced pumping function of the heart.<Cite n={[6, 7]} /> The valve is repaired or replaced, and a narrowed valve can sometimes be widened with a balloon during cardiac catheterisation.<Cite n={[7, 8]} /> In some people at high risk from conventional surgery, the valve may be replaced through a blood vessel using a catheter; this is decided by a team of doctors.<Cite n={6} />

If the aorta has widened a lot or keeps growing, its root and/or ascending part may be replaced with a graft.<Cite n={[7, 8]} /> Guidelines set aortic diameter thresholds for this planned operation. With some forms of widening, with risk factors for dissection, or if valve surgery is needed anyway, the threshold is lower. Doctors decide together with the person when to operate.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

Women with BAV and a widened aorta are advised to have the aorta imaged before pregnancy and to discuss the risk of dissection with their doctor.<Cite n={1} /> If the aorta is not widened and there is no Turner syndrome or heritable aortic disease, aortic dissection related to pregnancy is rarely reported.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, stenotic valves are treated with balloon dilation or surgical repair, and regurgitant valves with surgery.<Cite n={7} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline suggests considering transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in selected patients with BAV at high surgical risk, in high-volume TAVI centres, based on a decision of a multidisciplinary team.<Cite n={6} />

For replacement of the aortic root and/or ascending aorta in BAV, guidelines name a diameter of ≥ 55 mm (ACC/AHA: class 1; Russian Ministry of Health; ESC: I B), and for the root phenotype ESC names ≥ 50 mm (I B).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> With risk factors for dissection (see above), guidelines allow surgery from 50 mm (ACC/AHA: class 2a; Russian Ministry of Health; ESC: IIa C for the ascending phenotype at low surgical risk, with its own list of factors), and in patients undergoing aortic valve surgery from 45 mm (ACC/AHA: 2a; ESC: IIa C; Russian Ministry of Health: above 45 mm).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> Progressive aortic growth may occur after isolated aortic valve replacement, so surveillance continues.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

People with BAV and a dilated aorta are at risk of aortic dissection.<Cite n={1} /> With a thoracic aortic aneurysm, sudden severe pain may be a sign of a life-threatening emergency: call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK).<Cite n={3} /> The classic description of dissection pain is tearing or ripping, but more often people report the abrupt onset of severe sharp or stabbing pain in the chest or back (and sometimes the abdomen), worst at the very start.<Cite n={1} /> More about the warning signs is in the article on [aortic dissection](../aortic-dissection/).

A narrowed or leaking valve can cause fainting, shortness of breath and chest pain.<Cite n={8} /> The signs of severe valve narrowing are described in the article on [aortic stenosis](../aortic-stenosis/). As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
