Mitral stenosis is a narrowing of the mitral valve opening that obstructs the flow of blood from the left atrium into the left ventricle.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> The valve cannot open as widely as it should while the ventricle fills with blood (during diastole).<Cite n={2} /> The most common cause is past rheumatic fever.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

Mitral stenosis is most often a consequence of rheumatic fever or of frequent bouts of tonsillitis with pus (purulent tonsillitis) in childhood.<Cite n={2} /> Many people do not remember having had rheumatic fever.<Cite n={4} />

In rheumatic heart disease the valve leaflets thicken and fuse at their edges, and the thin cords (chordae) that hold them become shortened and matted together. The valve opening becomes narrow.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

In older people there is another cause: calcium builds up in the ring of the valve and spreads onto the leaflets, which become stiff and do not open fully.<Cite n={4} /> Occasionally the stenosis is present from birth or develops after radiation therapy.<Cite n={4} />

Because of the narrowing, blood has difficulty passing from the left atrium into the left ventricle. Pressure in the atrium rises, the atrium gradually enlarges, and the raised pressure is passed back to the blood vessels of the lungs.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Mitral stenosis (MS) is obstruction of left ventricular (LV) inflow at the level of the mitral valve (MV) due to structural deformity of the mitral apparatus, preventing adequate valve opening during diastolic filling of the LV.<Cite n={2} /> The most common cause is rheumatic carditis, although its incidence in industrialised countries has fallen considerably.<Cite n={2} />

The rheumatic process causes thickening and calcification of the leaflets and fusion of the commissures and chordae, producing a funnel-shaped mitral apparatus with a "fish-mouth" orifice.<Cite n={2} /> Calcific degeneration is increasingly observed in older patients: mitral annular calcification extends into the leaflets, and there is no commissural fusion.<Cite n={4} />

The normal MV area is 4.0–5.0 cm². As it decreases, flow from the left atrium (LA) to the LV creates a transmitral pressure gradient, the fundamental expression of MS, which raises LA pressure.<Cite n={2} /> LA size and pressure increase progressively; the rise in pulmonary venous and capillary pressures may cause secondary pulmonary hypertension.<Cite n={4} /> LA enlargement predisposes to atrial fibrillation (AF), a risk factor for thromboembolism.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

The disease can go without symptoms for a long time, and progresses faster in its later stages.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> While the valve opening is larger than 1.5 cm², there are usually no symptoms at rest.<Cite n={2} /> Symptoms usually appear many years after rheumatic fever.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

The main symptom is breathlessness: at first during physical activity, later also at rest; over time it becomes hard to breathe when lying flat.<Cite n={2} /> There may also be tiredness, a pounding or racing heartbeat, irregular heartbeats and chest pain.<Cite n={2} />

Symptoms may appear for the first time or get worse with physical activity, emotional stress, pregnancy, infection or atrial fibrillation with a fast heart rate.<Cite n={2} /> Many people have no symptoms until they become pregnant or atrial fibrillation develops.<Cite n={4} />

In an enlarged left atrium, especially with atrial fibrillation, blood pools and clots can form. A clot that breaks loose can block an artery and cause a stroke, a kidney infarction (death of part of a kidney after its blood supply is cut off) or a heart attack.<Cite n={2} /> Sometimes mitral stenosis first shows itself as atrial fibrillation or as a blood clot that travels through the bloodstream (thromboembolism).<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Initial symptoms are usually those of heart failure: exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea and fatigue.<Cite n={4} /> As the disease progresses there may also be cough, attacks of breathlessness, haemoptysis, dysphagia and hoarseness; oedema and heaviness in the right upper quadrant are typical of late stages.<Cite n={2} /> Haemoptysis is caused by rupture of small bronchial veins due to high LA pressure.<Cite n={2} />

The faster heart rate and loss of atrial contraction with onset of AF often lead to sudden worsening of symptoms.<Cite n={4} /> Systemic thromboembolism occurs in 10–20% of patients with MS; the risk increases with age and with AF.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A doctor may suspect stenosis when listening to the heart: typical findings are a loud first heart sound, a click as the valve opens and a low rumbling murmur.<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

The key test is an ultrasound scan of the heart (echocardiogram). It confirms the diagnosis and shows how narrow the valve is and how this has affected the heart.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> An ECG and a chest X-ray are usually done as well.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> Sometimes an ultrasound scan through the food pipe (transoesophageal echocardiogram) is needed: it shows small clots in the left atrium better.<Cite n={4} /> If there are no symptoms, the heart scan may be done during exercise to see how the heart copes with it.<Cite n={2} />

Stenosis needs regular check-ups.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> For mild stenosis, NICE advises considering a heart ultrasound scan every 3 to 5 years.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

On auscultation at the apex there is a loud, "snapping" first heart sound, an opening snap of the MV and a low-pitched rumbling mid-diastolic murmur; they may be absent with a heavily calcified immobile valve, severe pulmonary hypertension or low cardiac output.<Cite n={2} />

Transthoracic echocardiography is the key method for diagnosing MS and assessing its severity and haemodynamic consequences; it is recommended for all patients with suspected MS.<Cite n={2} /> MV area measured by planimetry is the reference measure of severity; mean transvalvular gradient and pulmonary artery pressure reflect the consequences of the stenosis and have prognostic value.<Cite n={2} />

The MSD Manual grades stenosis as moderate with a valve area of more than 1.5 and up to 2.5 cm², and as severe with an area of 1.5 cm² or less.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline grades severity with its own table, using mean gradient, valve area and systolic pulmonary artery pressure; the criteria apply at heart rates of 60 to 90 beats per minute.<Cite n={2} />

Transoesophageal echocardiography can detect or exclude small LA thrombi, especially in the LA appendage, which usually cannot be seen transthoracically.<Cite n={4} />

In sinus rhythm the most typical ECG findings are signs of right ventricular and LA hypertrophy (P mitrale).<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, people whose valve disease already causes symptoms need a procedure on the valve, not only medicines.<Cite n={2} /> When symptoms are mild, medicines can ease them: water tablets (diuretics) and, if the heart rate is fast, medicines that slow it.<Cite n={4} />

With atrial fibrillation, after a thromboembolism or with a clot in the atrium, medicines that reduce blood clotting (anticoagulants) are needed.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> Not all such medicines are suitable in mitral stenosis, so the choice is made by a doctor.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

If the valve is suitable, the narrowing can be widened without open surgery. A thin tube (catheter) with a balloon is passed through a vein into the heart, and the balloon is inflated in the valve to separate the fused leaflets.<Cite n={4} /> Guidelines advise this procedure (balloon commissurotomy, or valvuloplasty) for people with symptoms and marked narrowing of the valve, if there are no reasons it cannot be done.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> If the balloon procedure is not suitable, heart surgery is done: the valve is repaired or replaced with an artificial one.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

In rheumatic mitral stenosis, antibiotics are recommended to prevent recurrences of rheumatic fever.<Cite n={2} /> NICE advises offering family planning advice to women with severe mitral stenosis.<Cite n={3} />

The treatment approach is chosen by a team of specialists, taking into account the risk of the procedure and the experience of the hospital.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Drug therapy for chronic heart failure is recommended for all patients with severe MS.<Cite n={2} /> NICE suggests considering a beta-blocker for adults with moderate to severe MS and heart failure.<Cite n={3} />

Vitamin K antagonists are indicated to prevent thromboembolism in patients with AF, a history of embolism or an LA thrombus.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> According to the 2025 ESC guideline, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are not recommended in patients with AF and rheumatic MS with an MV area ≤2.0 cm² (class III, level B).<Cite n={1} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline likewise does not recommend DOACs for patients with moderate and severe MS.<Cite n={2} />

According to the 2025 ESC guideline, in clinically severe rheumatic or degenerative MS, percutaneous mitral commissurotomy (PMC) is recommended in symptomatic patients in the absence of unfavourable characteristics for PMC (I B), and MV surgery in symptomatic patients who are not suitable for PMC (I C).<Cite n={1} /> In clinically severe MS, PMC should also be considered in asymptomatic patients without unfavourable clinical and anatomical characteristics for PMC who have a high thromboembolic risk (for example, a history of systemic embolism, new-onset or paroxysmal AF) and/or a high risk of haemodynamic decompensation (for example, pregnancy or desire for pregnancy) (IIa C).<Cite n={1} />

Contraindications to PMC in rheumatic MS listed by the 2025 ESC guideline include an MV area greater than 1.5 cm², LA thrombus, more than mild mitral regurgitation, severe or bi-commissural calcification, and absence of commissural fusion.<Cite n={1} /> NICE suggests considering transcatheter valvotomy for adults with rheumatic severe MS if the valve is suitable for this procedure, and recommends offering surgical MV replacement if transcatheter valvotomy is unsuitable.<Cite n={3} />

When the cause is mitral annular calcification, balloon commissurotomy gives no benefit because there is no commissural fusion.<Cite n={4} /> After balloon or surgical commissurotomy, valve function deteriorates over time in most patients due to restenosis, and valve replacement may become necessary.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## When to seek urgent medical help

Mitral stenosis is a long-term condition, but it can suddenly get worse. When the valve disease decompensates, fluid can build up in the lungs (pulmonary oedema), and patients are admitted to hospital.<Cite n={2} /> Atrial fibrillation with a poorly controlled fast heart rate can cause pulmonary oedema and sudden severe breathlessness.<Cite n={4} /> Sometimes mitral stenosis first shows itself as an embolism, such as a stroke.<Cite n={4} /> Pulmonary oedema caused by decompensation and an attack of atrial fibrillation are reasons for emergency hospital admission.<Cite n={2} /> So if severe breathlessness comes on suddenly, especially at rest or when lying flat, or an attack of fast, irregular heartbeat begins, call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK).<Cite n={[2, 4]} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline advises people with mitral stenosis to see a cardiologist without waiting for their next scheduled visit if, among other things, tiredness, breathlessness, swelling, an enlarging abdomen or heart rhythm problems appear or get much worse, or if they develop an acute respiratory infection or a fever of unknown cause.<Cite n={2} />

If there are signs of a stroke (sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, a drooping face), call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away. As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
