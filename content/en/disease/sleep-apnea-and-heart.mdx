Obstructive sleep apnoea is a condition in which the upper airway repeatedly collapses at the level of the throat (pharynx) during sleep and breathing stops, even though the efforts to breathe continue.<Cite n={4} /> As a result, the oxygen level in the blood falls, sleep is broken up, and marked sleepiness follows during the day.<Cite n={4} /> The pauses in breathing usually end with a brief awakening.<Cite n={7} />

Sleep apnoea matters for the heart: without treatment it raises the risk of high blood pressure, arrhythmias and heart failure.<Cite n={7} /> This article is about how sleep apnoea is linked to the heart. The conditions themselves are covered in more detail in the articles on [hypertension](../hypertension/), [atrial fibrillation](../atrial-fibrillation/) and [heart failure](../heart-failure/).

## Why it happens

<Patient>

During sleep the muscle tone of the throat decreases, and in people with sleep apnoea the throat becomes partly or completely blocked; the blood then holds less oxygen and more carbon dioxide.<Cite n={7} /> Trying to breathe in against a closed airway causes swings in pressure inside the chest that affect how the heart works.<Cite n={7} />

The risk of sleep apnoea is raised by obesity, differences in the structure of the upper airway, sleep apnoea in relatives, some conditions (for example, an underactive thyroid or a previous stroke), and alcohol or sedatives.<Cite n={7} /> Sleep apnoea becomes more common with age and in women after the menopause.<Cite n={7} /> But not everyone with sleep apnoea is overweight.<Cite n={7} />

How sleep apnoea affects the heart and blood vessels:

- In sleep apnoea, especially when it is severe, the sympathetic nervous system is activated. Blood pressure at night rises or does not fall enough, the inner lining of the blood vessels is affected, and the risk of cardiovascular complications goes up.<Cite n={4} /> People with untreated sleep apnoea and normal blood pressure are more likely to develop high blood pressure.<Cite n={7} />
- Sleep apnoea can contribute to atrial fibrillation: it raises the pressure in the atria, enlarges them or changes how the autonomic nervous system works.<Cite n={5} />
- Lack of oxygen during the pauses increases the tone of the vagus nerve, so the pulse often slows during sleep; conduction disturbances also occur.<Cite n={2} /> But a slow pulse at night also happens in people without sleep apnoea: most often it is harmless and needs no treatment.<Cite n={2} />
- Repeated lack of oxygen at night and broken sleep are linked to a higher risk of heart failure, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation and stroke.<Cite n={7} />

</Patient>

According to NICE, an apnoea is a complete pause in breathing and a hypopnoea is a reduction in breathing, each lasting 10 seconds or more, and the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) is their number per hour of a multi-channel sleep study.<Cite n={6} />

OSA leads to hypoxia, hypercapnia, and endothelial and neurotransmitter dysfunction.<Cite n={7} /> Russian Ministry of Health hypertension guideline: in OSA, especially when severe, sympathetic activation leads to a rise or an insufficient fall in night-time blood pressure, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress and a higher risk of cardiovascular complications.<Cite n={4} /> Russian Ministry of Health AF guideline: OSA, especially combined with hypertension, diabetes and structural heart disease, may be a pathogenetic factor of AF.<Cite n={5} /> The 2024 ESC guidelines call OSA a highly prevalent condition, particularly in patients with AF, and list sleep apnoea among the factors associated with incident AF.<Cite n={1} />

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Sleep apnoea may be suspected from loud, broken snoring and pauses in breathing during sleep, restless, unrefreshing sleep, waking often at night to pass urine, daytime sleepiness, feeling worn out and having headaches in the morning, constant tiredness, and poorer memory and concentration.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> Some people have episodes of choking during sleep.<Cite n={6} /> Some only learn about their night-time symptoms from the people they live with.<Cite n={7} />

Snoring on its own does not mean sleep apnoea: most people who snore do not have it.<Cite n={7} /> And the other way round, not everyone with sleep apnoea feels very sleepy,<Cite n={6} /> and in some people even moderate or severe sleep apnoea causes hardly any symptoms.<Cite n={7} />

Daytime sleepiness is dangerous: untreated sleep apnoea raises the risk of injury and death in accidents when a person falls asleep at the wheel or during another hazardous activity.<Cite n={7} />

</Patient>

Among the features that suggest OSA, the Russian Ministry of Health hypertension guideline also lists weight gain and reduced potency.<Cite n={4} /> NICE recommends assessing people for OSAHS if they have 2 or more of its typical features.<Cite n={6} />

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A doctor suspects sleep apnoea from the symptoms and risk factors, and a sleep study confirms it.<Cite n={7} /> Questionnaires, such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, help to judge how likely sleep apnoea is, but they are not used to make the diagnosis.<Cite n={[4, 6, 7]} />

To make the diagnosis and assess severity, breathing is recorded during sleep (respiratory or cardiorespiratory monitoring); the UK NICE guideline offers this recording at home.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> The "gold standard" is polysomnography, a detailed recording of sleep; it is used in particular when the home recording did not show sleep apnoea but the symptoms continue.<Cite n={[4, 6]} />

People with heart conditions are checked for sleep apnoea with particular care:

- in hypertension, if there is obesity, if blood pressure is hard to control with treatment, or if 24-hour blood pressure monitoring shows that blood pressure does not fall or even rises at night;<Cite n={4} />
- in atrial fibrillation, if sleep apnoea is highly likely;<Cite n={5} />
- with a markedly slow pulse or serious conduction block during sleep, if there are signs of sleep apnoea.<Cite n={2} /> Rhythm and conduction problems are covered in the articles on [sick sinus syndrome](../sick-sinus-syndrome/) and [atrioventricular block](../av-block/).

</Patient>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

Everyone with sleep apnoea is advised to change their lifestyle: lose weight if they have obesity, stop smoking, drink less alcohol, and improve their sleep and free breathing through the nose; the doctor will also assess whether tranquillisers and sleeping pills are needed.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> Losing weight reduces the severity of sleep apnoea but does not necessarily get rid of it.<Cite n={7} /> Do not stop prescribed medicines on your own: discuss them with your doctor.

The main treatment is CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure): a machine delivers air through a mask at a constant positive pressure, and this airflow stops the upper airway from collapsing.<Cite n={4} /> CPAP reduces sleepiness and snoring and improves the bed partner's sleep.<Cite n={7} /> It works while it is used: if it is stopped, the symptoms return within a few days, and it is usually continued indefinitely.<Cite n={7} /> Many people find it hard to use the machine regularly, especially those who do not feel sleepy.<Cite n={7} />

If CPAP is not suitable, the doctor may offer other options: a mouth splint, a device to avoid sleeping on the back, or surgery.<Cite n={[6, 7]} /> The doctor chooses the method together with the patient.

What treating sleep apnoea does for the heart:

- In people with high blood pressure and sleep apnoea, the Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommends CPAP to control blood pressure better.<Cite n={4} /> But blood pressure usually falls only modestly.<Cite n={7} />
- In atrial fibrillation, treating sleep apnoea is part of managing risk factors, so that episodes come back less often.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> The evidence on the benefit of CPAP here is mixed.<Cite n={[1, 7]} />
- If the pulse slows during sleep because of confirmed sleep apnoea, the sleep apnoea is treated, with CPAP and weight loss, and a pacemaker is avoided where possible.<Cite n={2} /> If a slow pulse with symptoms also happens outside sleep, the doctor weighs the risks of the slow pulse against the benefit of a pacemaker.<Cite n={2} />

Before any operation, tell the anaesthetist that you have sleep apnoea.<Cite n={7} /> Until treatment is in place, people with marked daytime sleepiness are warned about the risks of driving and of work where falling asleep would be dangerous.<Cite n={7} />

</Patient>

NICE: offer fixed-level CPAP, in addition to lifestyle advice, to people with moderate or severe OSAHS, and if they cannot tolerate or decline CPAP, consider a mandibular advancement splint; consider tonsillectomy for people with large obstructive tonsils and a BMI below 35 kg/m².<Cite n={6} /> According to MSD, treatment of OSA is associated with modest 2 to 3 mm Hg decreases in blood pressure, but data are mixed on the prevention of cardiovascular events and AF.<Cite n={7} />

## When to call an ambulance

Emergencies that sleep apnoea is linked to are described in the articles on [atrial fibrillation](../atrial-fibrillation/), [atrioventricular block](../av-block/), [sick sinus syndrome](../sick-sinus-syndrome/), [hypertensive crisis](../hypertensive-crisis/) and [heart failure](../heart-failure/): they list the signs that need urgent help.

As with any heart or blood vessel condition, call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) for fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
