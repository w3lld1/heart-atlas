Subclavian artery stenosis is a narrowing of the artery that carries blood to the arm and, through its branches, to the neck and brain.<Cite n={7} /> It can be caused by atherosclerosis: when atherosclerosis affects the arteries of the arms, it is most often the subclavian artery, especially the first part of the left one.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> The narrowing can cause arm fatigue during work.<Cite n={5} /> If it lies before the point where the vertebral artery branches off, blood can flow to the working arm out of the vertebral artery, which supplies the brain; this is called subclavian steal syndrome.<Cite n={6} /> A difference in blood pressure between the right and left arm helps to suspect the narrowing.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

The subclavian artery gives off the vertebral artery, which rises along the neck vertebrae to the brain and spinal cord, and the internal thoracic (internal mammary) artery, which is often used in coronary artery bypass surgery.<Cite n={7} />

The subclavian artery can be narrowed by atherosclerosis.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> The risk factors are the same as for atherosclerosis in general: smoking, diabetes, high cholesterol and high blood pressure.<Cite n={5} /> More about related conditions in the articles on [carotid artery stenosis](../carotid-artery-stenosis/), [peripheral artery disease](../peripheral-artery-disease/), [dyslipidaemia](../dyslipidemia/) and [high blood pressure](../hypertension/).

Apart from atherosclerosis, a sudden blockage of the subclavian artery by a clot can be caused by injury to the artery, compression where it leaves the chest, inflammation of the artery wall (vasculitis), or a clot carried in the bloodstream from the heart or from arteries further upstream.<Cite n={7} /> One vasculitis that narrows the branches of the aortic arch is described in the article on [Takayasu arteritis](../takayasu-arteritis/).

</Patient>

<Student>

Subclavian steal syndrome (MSD): a subclavian artery stenosed proximal to the origin of the vertebral artery "steals" blood from the vertebral artery, in which flow reverses, to supply the arm during exertion, causing signs of vertebrobasilar ischaemia.<Cite n={6} /> When the subclavian artery is narrowed or occluded, blood from the contralateral vertebral artery or other intracranial collaterals can flow retrogradely through the vertebrobasilar system; this collateral route helps maintain brain perfusion and lowers the risk of ischaemia.<Cite n={7} />

Coronary-subclavian steal concerns patients after CABG whose ipsilateral internal mammary artery has been used as a graft to the coronary arteries: with a proximal subclavian stenosis they may develop myocardial ischaemia.<Cite n={1} /> On myocardial ischaemia, see [coronary heart disease](../coronary-heart-disease/).

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

The narrowing can cause arm fatigue during exertion, similar to intermittent claudication.<Cite n={5} /> Occasionally small particles break off the plaque and block arteries of the hand.<Cite n={5} />

In subclavian steal, working with the arm can bring on symptoms of poor blood supply to the back of the brain: dizziness, unsteadiness, double vision, difficulty speaking or swallowing.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> When the start of the subclavian artery is blocked, there may also be fainting, visual disturbances and sometimes a transient ischaemic attack or stroke.<Cite n={7} />

Sudden pain, numbness, weakness, coldness and paleness of the arm are signs of an acute blockage of the artery, which is an emergency; more in the article on [acute limb ischaemia](../acute-limb-ischemia/).<Cite n={[3, 7]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Vertebrobasilar TIA (MSD): unilateral or bilateral cranial nerve deficits such as nystagmus, vertigo, dysphagia, dysarthria and diplopia; truncal or limb ataxia.<Cite n={6} /> Proximal subclavian artery thrombosis (StatPearls) may produce neurological symptoms related to steal physiology: dizziness, syncope, visual disturbances and posterior circulation ischaemic events.<Cite n={7} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

The European guideline advises measuring blood pressure in both arms in everyone with diseases of the arteries and aorta.<Cite n={1} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline on hypertension advises measuring blood pressure in both arms at least at the first visit so as not to miss arterial disease; after that, blood pressure is measured in the arm with the higher reading.<Cite n={2} /> The difference is judged by a doctor: it is not a self-test with a home blood pressure monitor.

The first test is an ultrasound (duplex) scan of the arteries.<Cite n={[1, 7]} /> CT and MR angiography give a detailed picture, and catheter angiography is done when treatment from inside the vessel is planned.<Cite n={7} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ESC 2024: bilateral arm BP measurement is recommended for all patients with peripheral arterial and aortic diseases (I B).<Cite n={1} /> Atherosclerotic upper-extremity artery disease may be suspected from an absolute inter-arm SBP difference of more than 10–15 mmHg; duplex ultrasound is the first-line imaging test.<Cite n={1} /> Russian Ministry of Health guideline 62_3: an inter-arm BP difference of more than 15 mmHg suggests probable atherosclerotic vascular disease.<Cite n={2} /> The thresholds differ between guidelines.

CTA and MRA give a detailed anatomical assessment of the lesion, including collaterals, branch involvement and suitability for intervention; catheter angiography remains the reference standard when endovascular treatment is planned.<Cite n={7} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

For an atherosclerotic narrowing the basis of treatment is the same as for other atherosclerotic diseases of the arteries and aorta: stopping smoking completely, in any form, a healthy diet, physical activity and cholesterol-lowering medicines.<Cite n={1} /> High blood pressure is treated.<Cite n={1} /> Which medicines are needed is for the doctor to decide.

If the narrowing causes no symptoms, restoring blood flow by surgery or through a catheter "just in case" is not recommended.<Cite n={1} /> If there are symptoms (for example, a TIA or stroke, coronary-subclavian steal after heart bypass surgery, a dialysis access in that arm that works poorly, or a severe lack of blood in the arm), a vascular team decides case by case how to restore blood flow.<Cite n={1} /> The artery can be widened from inside through a catheter (with a balloon, with or without a stent) or treated with open surgery.<Cite n={[1, 7]} /> The European guideline allows catheter treatment to be preferred because it has fewer complications.<Cite n={1} />

If a person is going to have heart bypass surgery using the internal mammary artery, doctors may consider treating a narrowing at the start of the subclavian artery on the same side.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ESC 2024, recommendations for subclavian artery stenosis: routine revascularisation in atherosclerotic subclavian artery disease is not recommended (III C); in symptomatic patients (TIA/stroke, coronary-subclavian steal syndrome, ipsilateral haemodialysis access dysfunction, severe ischaemia) both revascularisation options, endovascular (± stenting) and surgical, should be considered and discussed case by case by a vascular team (IIa B); endovascular revascularisation may be considered over surgery, despite similar long-term outcomes, due to lower complication rates (IIb B).<Cite n={1} /> Revascularisation should be considered for proximal stenosis in patients undergoing CABG using the ipsilateral internal mammary artery; in patients who already have the ipsilateral IMA grafted to the coronary arteries with evidence of myocardial ischaemia; and with an ipsilateral haemodialysis arteriovenous access (all IIa C).<Cite n={1} />

Open options, carotid-subclavian bypass and subclavian-carotid transposition, are used for extensive occlusion, failed endovascular therapy, arterial compression syndromes or anatomy unsuitable for minimally invasive intervention.<Cite n={7} /> For treatment of acute thrombosis, see [acute limb ischaemia](../acute-limb-ischemia/).

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

Call an ambulance straight away on your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if there are signs of stroke: sudden weakness of the face, an arm or a leg, especially on one side of the body, numbness, or difficulty speaking or understanding speech.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline describes a quick test to recognise a stroke: ask the person to smile, to speak and to raise both arms. If they suddenly cannot do one of these, call an ambulance straight away, describe the symptoms and tell the paramedics when they began.<Cite n={4} /> Also call an ambulance for sudden dizziness with unsteadiness, double vision, or difficulty speaking or swallowing: these may be signs of poor blood supply to the back of the brain.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> If the symptoms went away quickly, it may have been a TIA: after it the risk of stroke rises sharply within the first day, so the person needs to be assessed quickly, usually in hospital.<Cite n={6} /> So call an ambulance even if everything has passed.

Also call an ambulance if an arm suddenly becomes cold, pale and painful, numb or weak, and its pulse has gone: this may be an acute blockage of the artery, and the person needs urgent assessment in hospital.<Cite n={[3, 7]} />

Arm fatigue during work and a difference in blood pressure between the arms that you have noticed are reasons to see a doctor soon. If you feel dizzy when working with your arm, also see a doctor soon; but if unsteadiness, double vision, or difficulty speaking or swallowing appear, call an ambulance, even if everything has passed.

As with any heart or blood vessel condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
