Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a disorder of the ion channels of heart cells in which the electrical activation of the ventricular cells lasts longer than usual and the QT interval on the [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) is prolonged.<Cite n={2} /> It can be congenital, acquired, or both.<Cite n={2} /> Its main danger is a [ventricular tachycardia](../ventricular-tachycardia/) called torsades de pointes, which may stop on its own or turn into ventricular fibrillation.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> Congenital LQTS is the most common inherited channelopathy: the Russian Ministry of Health guideline gives a frequency of 1 in 2,500–3,000 people, and the MSD Manual at least 1 in 2,000.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

Congenital LQTS is caused by changes (mutations) in genes, most of which control ion channels in heart cells: potassium, sodium or calcium channels.<Cite n={1} /> It runs in families, so relatives are checked too (see the section on diagnosis).<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

In the acquired form, the dangerous arrhythmia is most often triggered by medicines that prolong the QT interval: some medicines for arrhythmias and many medicines prescribed for conditions other than heart disease, such as some antidepressants, antivirals and antifungals.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> The risk of this arrhythmia is raised by female sex, older age, low potassium or magnesium in the blood, an underactive thyroid, a slow or irregular heart rate, major brain damage (bleeding, stroke, head injury), eating disorders and heart disease; usually there are several causes.<Cite n={3} /> Potassium and magnesium can drop with diarrhoea, vomiting or metabolic disorders.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

QTc prolongation reflects a longer ventricular myocyte action potential: in the congenital forms this is loss of function of repolarising potassium channels or gain of function of depolarising sodium or calcium channels.<Cite n={2} /> A prolonged action potential makes early afterdepolarisations more likely; if action potential duration varies between neighbouring areas, these can reactivate myocytes that have already repolarised and start torsades de pointes.<Cite n={2} />

Mutations in 17 genes have been described; genetic screening finds a pathological mutation in 75% of LQTS cases in three main genes, KCNQ1, KCNH2 and SCN5A (types 1, 2 and 3), which make up 90% of genotype-positive cases.<Cite n={1} /> These three forms are inherited as autosomal dominant disorders with incomplete penetrance.<Cite n={2} /> Forms with extracardiac features include Andersen–Tawil and Timothy syndromes; autosomal recessive Jervell and Lange-Nielsen syndrome combines extreme QT prolongation with congenital deafness.<Cite n={1} />

The most common predisposing factor in acquired torsades de pointes ventricular tachycardia (VT) is a QT-prolonging medicine, especially a class Ia, Ic or III antiarrhythmic, as well as tricyclic antidepressants, phenothiazines and certain antivirals and antifungals.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

As long as there is no arrhythmia, the syndrome causes no symptoms.<Cite n={2} /> If torsades de pointes starts, it can cause palpitations, feeling faint, fainting or sudden death.<Cite n={2} /> Some people have muscle jerks during a faint, and epilepsy may be wrongly suspected.<Cite n={2} />

Doctors think of LQTS when a person, especially a young one, faints at the peak of physical or emotional stress, or with a sudden noise, while swimming or on waking.<Cite n={1} /> Symptoms first appear at an average age of 14.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Without treatment the annual risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in LQTS is 0.3–0.9%, and syncope occurs in 5% of patients a year.<Cite n={1} /> MSD links different triggers to the types: type 1, physical exertion, particularly swimming, or emotional stress; type 2, sudden loud noises such as an alarm clock; type 3, rest or sleep.<Cite n={2} /> Neither arrhythmia triggers nor ECG findings are specific enough to confirm the type or to direct genetic testing.<Cite n={2} />

Torsades de pointes often causes syncope because at a rate of 200–250 beats a minute it does not maintain perfusion; palpitations are common in patients who stay conscious.<Cite n={3} /> Because the ventricular action potential shortens as the heart rate rises, torsades is often self-terminating, but it may degenerate into ventricular fibrillation and cause cardiac arrest.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

The main test is an ECG: the doctor measures the QT interval and corrects it for heart rate.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> Sometimes a long QT is found by chance on an ECG done for another reason.<Cite n={2} /> One normal ECG does not rule out the congenital syndrome: the QTc is normal in 25–50% of people with a confirmed mutation.<Cite n={2} /> So an ECG during and after exercise, [Holter monitoring](../../diagnostic/holter-monitoring/) and a genetic test may be needed.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

If the QT is long, the doctor looks for reversible causes: potassium and calcium levels, thyroid hormones, the medicines the person takes and their diet.<Cite n={1} /> The doctor will also ask whether any relatives died suddenly and unexpectedly at a young age, for example by drowning.<Cite n={1} /> If a relative died suddenly and a post-mortem found no clear cause, the closest relatives are advised to be examined by a cardiologist.<Cite n={1} />

If a mutation is found, all close relatives are tested for it, even if they have no symptoms and a normal ECG.<Cite n={1} /> First-degree relatives have a clinical assessment and an ECG; then the first-degree relatives of each newly identified person are assessed in the same way.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The Russian guideline quotes the criteria of the 2022 ESC expert panel: LQTS is diagnosed with a QTc ≥ 480 ms or a Schwartz score > 3 points; after arrhythmic syncope or cardiac arrest a QTc ≥ 460 ms is enough.<Cite n={1} /> The Schwartz score (Appendix G1 of the guideline) gives points for the ECG (for example, QTc ≥ 480 ms 3.5 points, 460–479 ms 2), syncope (with exertion 2, without exertion 1), family history and a pathogenic mutation (3.5 points).<Cite n={1} />

Molecular genetic testing (KCNQ1, KCNH2, SCN5A, CALM1, CALM2, CALM3) is recommended for all patients with a high probability of a clinical diagnosis; mutations are found in about 75%, so a negative result does not rule out LQTS.<Cite n={1} /> Variant-specific cascade family screening is recommended for the proband's relatives; for children who are relatives of a proband with a known mutation, genetic testing is recommended from birth.<Cite n={1} />

Thresholds for a "normal" QTc differ between sources.<Cite n={[1, 2, 4]} /> When an ECG is read manually after transient loss of consciousness in people over 16, NICE treats a corrected QT of more than 450 ms as long QT; in a person who has had a blackout, this finding is a reason for urgent referral for cardiovascular assessment.<Cite n={4} /> This is a threshold for the initial assessment of a blackout, whereas the Russian guideline diagnoses LQTS by the criteria above.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The Russian guideline advises everyone with LQTS to avoid medicines that prolong the QT interval, to have abnormal potassium, magnesium and calcium levels corrected and to avoid arrhythmia triggers: vigorous swimming (especially in type 1) and loud noises in type 2.<Cite n={1} /> If a person is already taking a medicine that raises the risk, the doctor stops it where possible and prescribes an alternative.<Cite n={2} />

To prevent sudden death, the Russian guideline recommends beta-blockers for everyone with LQTS.<Cite n={1} /> They are also recommended for women with LQTS during pregnancy and after giving birth.<Cite n={1} /> People who have survived a cardiac arrest are advised to have an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) together with beta-blockers: this device delivers a shock to stop ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation.<Cite n={1} /> An ICD is also recommended for people who faint despite taking beta-blockers.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> If a person has symptoms and beta-blockers do not work or are not suitable, an ICD cannot be used or the person declines it, or ICD shocks keep happening, an operation called left cardiac sympathetic denervation is recommended.<Cite n={1} />

Strenuous exercise in types 1 and 2 is discussed carefully with the doctor; people who choose to play sport are advised, for example, to have an automated external defibrillator available during training and competition.<Cite n={2} /> In the acquired form, the medicine that caused the long QT is stopped, and after that the main thing is to avoid anything that prolongs the QT interval.<Cite n={3} /> The doctor and the patient decide together which approach to take.

</Patient>

<Student>

Beta-blockers are recommended to prevent SCD in all patients with LQTS (ESC IB), with non-selective beta-blockers preferred; they are also recommended for mutation carriers with a normal QT interval (ESC IIaB).<Cite n={1} /> An ICD in combination with beta-blockers is recommended for survivors of cardiac arrest (ESC IB) and for patients with syncope and/or VT on an adequate dose of beta-blockers (ESC IIaC).<Cite n={1} /> Left cardiac sympathetic denervation is recommended in symptomatic patients when beta-blockers are ineffective or unusable, an ICD is contraindicated or declined, or multiple ICD shocks continue.<Cite n={1} />

In polymorphic torsades de pointes VT, the causes of QT prolongation are identified and removed: QT-prolonging medicines are stopped, electrolytes corrected and bradycardia eliminated.<Cite n={1} /> Intravenous magnesium is recommended to prevent recurrences; if it does not work, adults have temporary pacing at a high rate to shorten the QT, and class IA and III antiarrhythmics are not recommended to stop the arrhythmia.<Cite n={1} /> If it degenerates into ventricular fibrillation, unsynchronised defibrillation is given.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

Torsades de pointes can turn into ventricular fibrillation and cause sudden death.<Cite n={3} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline advises calling an ambulance if an episode of arrhythmia comes with dizziness, weakness, feeling faint, loss of consciousness or a racing heartbeat, and also for any episode of feeling faint or losing consciousness.<Cite n={1} /> An ambulance is also called when an implanted cardioverter-defibrillator has gone off.<Cite n={1} /> Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK).

Even if the person has come round, NICE (for people over 16) treats the following as red flags after a blackout: an ECG abnormality, including a long QT interval, a blackout during exertion, and a family history of sudden cardiac death in people younger than 40 or an inherited heart condition: such a person is referred urgently for cardiovascular assessment, and a specialist reviews the referral within 24 hours.<Cite n={4} />
