A thoracic aortic aneurysm is a permanent widening of the <Structure id="aorta">aorta</Structure> above the diaphragm.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> It can occur in the aortic root, the ascending aorta, the arch or the descending thoracic aorta, and several segments are often widened at once.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> If an aneurysm involves both the descending thoracic aorta and the abdominal aorta, it is called thoracoabdominal.<Cite n={2} /> Most aneurysms cause no symptoms, but the larger the diameter, the higher the risk of aortic dissection and rupture.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> [Abdominal aortic aneurysm](../abdominal-aortic-aneurysm/) and [aortic dissection](../aortic-dissection/) are covered in separate articles.

There is no single size at which a widening is called an aneurysm. The Russian Ministry of Health guideline and the MSD Manual define an aneurysm as a widening of the aorta by more than 50% compared with its normal diameter.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> The American ACC/AHA guideline gives the same conventional definition (at least 1.5 times the expected diameter) but says that it fails for the aortic root and ascending aorta: there it calls an aorta of 4.0–4.4 cm dilated, and one of 4.5 cm or more an aneurysm.<Cite n={3} /> Under the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, an aortic diameter of less than 4.0 cm is normal, and a widening above normal that does not yet meet the criteria for an aneurysm is called borderline dilatation.<Cite n={2} /> The size of the aorta depends on sex, age, height and weight.<Cite n={[2, 3]} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

The causes vary: inherited diseases, conditions present from birth, age-related changes in the aortic wall, inflammation and infection. An aneurysm can also remain after an aortic dissection.<Cite n={[2, 3]} />

Aneurysms of the root and ascending aorta are often inherited and appear at a younger age. They are not uncommon in people with a bicuspid <Structure id="aortic-valve">aortic valve</Structure>.<Cite n={2} /> Aneurysms of the descending thoracic aorta are more often linked to atherosclerosis and occur in middle-aged and older people.<Cite n={[2, 3]} />

About one in five thoracic aortic aneurysms has an inherited cause.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> Such diseases include [Marfan syndrome](../marfan-syndrome/) and Ehlers–Danlos, Turner and Loeys–Dietz syndromes.<Cite n={2} /> High blood pressure, smoking and high cholesterol also raise the risk.<Cite n={[3, 4]} />

The main danger of an aneurysm is aortic dissection and rupture. The larger the diameter and the faster the aneurysm grows, the higher this risk.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> But a smaller aneurysm can also rupture, especially in inherited connective tissue diseases.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The main aetiological groups of thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) and thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) are heritable disorders, congenital aortopathies, multifactorial degenerative conditions, and inflammatory and infectious diseases; aneurysmal dilatation is often the outcome of aortic dissection.<Cite n={[2, 3]} />

Aneurysms of the aortic root and ascending aorta are diagnosed at a younger age than aneurysms of the descending thoracic aorta (at 60 versus 72 years, respectively); the former tend to have a congenital or hereditary cause, the latter an atherosclerotic one (ACC/AHA).<Cite n={3} /> The extent of a TAAA is described by the Crawford classification as modified by Safi; it also helps predict morbidity and mortality associated with repair.<Cite n={2} />

The incidence of TAA is 5–10 cases per 100,000 person-years.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, the disease is most typical at the age of 50–70 years.<Cite n={2} /> About 20% of TAAs are related to a heritable condition; up to 20% of patients with TAA or aortic dissection have at least one first-degree relative with thoracic aortic disease (ACC/AHA).<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> Patients with TAA have a modestly increased incidence of abdominal aortic aneurysms and cerebral aneurysms.<Cite n={3} />

Growth rates, according to ACC/AHA: with a tricuspid aortic valve and without a genetic aortic disorder, the ascending aorta frequently grows by less than 0.05 cm/year; arch aneurysms by about 0.25 cm/year; descending aneurysms and TAAA by a mean of 0.19 cm/year, faster at larger diameters; the aortic root in Marfan syndrome by a mean of 0.26 cm/year.<Cite n={3} /> Rapid growth (≥0.5 cm/year), symptomatic aneurysm, an underlying connective tissue disorder or heritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD), saccular morphology, female sex and infected aneurysm are associated with a higher tendency for rupture (the Russian Ministry of Health guideline and ACC/AHA, in their sections on the descending thoracic aorta).<Cite n={[2, 3]} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Most thoracic aortic aneurysms cause no symptoms and can become very large without any.<Cite n={[2, 5]} /> They are often found by chance, on a chest CT scan done for another reason.<Cite n={5} />

A large aneurysm can press on nearby organs. This can cause cough, shortness of breath, a hoarse voice (from pressure on a nerve to the voice box), difficult or painful swallowing (from pressure on the gullet), swelling of the arms (from pressure on large veins), pain or discomfort in the chest, and a feeling of "pulsation" in the chest.<Cite n={2} /> There may be back pain and wheezing.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> Pain may be felt in the jaw, neck or upper back.<Cite n={2} />

If an aneurysm starts to hurt, it is a warning sign: pain is sometimes linked to rapid growth of the aneurysm, and its appearance raises concern about a higher risk of rupture.<Cite n={3} />

Widening of the aorta next to the heart can stop the aortic valve from closing properly, so some blood leaks back into the heart; this is [aortic regurgitation](../aortic-regurgitation/).<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Symptoms of compression of surrounding structures: back pain (vertebral compression), cough or wheezing (tracheobronchial tree), dysphagia (oesophagus), hoarseness (left recurrent laryngeal or vagus nerve).<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline adds upper limb oedema from compression of the superior vena cava or brachiocephalic vein.<Cite n={2} /> For arch aneurysms, ACC/AHA describes nonspecific symptoms: chest pain or pressure, fatigue, and neck, jaw or back pain.<Cite n={3} />

Ascending aortic aneurysms sometimes involve the aortic root and cause aortic regurgitation.<Cite n={4} /> A ruptured TAA that is not immediately fatal presents with severe chest or back pain and hypotension or shock.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

An ordinary chest X-ray may suggest an aneurysm, but it is not a reliable way to find one.<Cite n={4} /> The first test is an ultrasound of the heart (echocardiography). It shows the root and ascending aorta well, as well as the structure and function of the aortic valve.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> But part of the ascending aorta and the arch cannot be seen on ultrasound.<Cite n={2} />

To measure the aorta accurately and examine all of it, doctors use CT angiography (computed tomography with contrast) or MR angiography.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> MR angiography involves no radiation, so it is well suited to repeated scans in young people.<Cite n={2} /> If an aneurysm is found in one place, the whole aorta is checked.<Cite n={1} /> To compare sizes correctly, repeat scans measure the aorta with the same method and in the same way.<Cite n={1} />

Aneurysms of the root and ascending aorta can be inherited, so for them guidelines recommend checking the aorta in first-degree relatives: parents, brothers and sisters, and children.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> If there are signs of an inherited disease, the doctor may refer the person to a geneticist.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

A small aneurysm is usually not operated on but monitored: scans are repeated regularly to see whether it is growing.<Cite n={[2, 3, 5]} /> How often they are done depends on the size of the aneurysm, its location and its cause.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

Treatment without surgery aims to slow the growth of the aneurysm and prevent complications: dissection and rupture.<Cite n={2} /> If you smoke, quitting is very important.<Cite n={[2, 3, 5]} /> High blood pressure needs treatment; which medicines are needed and how far to lower the pressure is decided by the doctor.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> Guidelines advise avoiding heavy lifting, straining exercises (such as heavy weightlifting) and contact sports.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> Light or moderate aerobic exercise is recommended when blood pressure is at target,<Cite n={2} /> and how much to do is discussed with the doctor.<Cite n={1} /> According to ESC, antibiotics of the fluoroquinolone group are generally not given to people with an aortic aneurysm if other suitable options exist, so it is important that the treating doctor knows about the aneurysm.<Cite n={1} />

It is better to treat an aneurysm before it ruptures, so surgery is offered in advance: when the aorta reaches a certain size, grows quickly or the aneurysm causes symptoms.<Cite n={[2, 3, 5]} /> The thresholds differ for different segments of the aorta and between guidelines. In Marfan syndrome rupture is more likely, so surgery may be done at a smaller size.<Cite n={5} /> When and how to operate is decided by doctors together with the patient.<Cite n={3} />

The ascending aorta and the arch (and, if the root is widened, the root too) are usually replaced with a synthetic graft during open surgery through an incision in the breastbone.<Cite n={4} /> When the root is widened, experienced centres sometimes keep the person's own aortic valve.<Cite n={1} /> If its shape is suitable, the descending thoracic aorta is more often treated endovascularly: a stent graft, a tube of fabric on a metal frame, is passed through an artery in the groin up to the aneurysm and opened inside the aorta so that blood flows through it.<Cite n={[1, 3, 5]} /> Operations on the arch are more difficult because the blood supply to the brain must be preserved.<Cite n={3} /> Complications are possible, including stroke and spinal cord injury, so these operations are best done in experienced centres.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> After surgery, regular CT scans are needed, and after a stent graft they are needed for life.<Cite n={[1, 2, 4]} />

</Patient>

Surveillance. ACC/AHA and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline: repeat imaging 6–12 months after detection to determine the rate of growth, and if the size is stable, every 6–24 months depending on the diameter.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> ESC: annually if there is no expansion, or on an individual schedule (IIa C); for aneurysms of the distal ascending aorta, arch, descending aorta or TAAA, CT or MRI (I C), while TTE is not recommended for surveillance of the distal ascending aorta, arch and descending aorta (III C).<Cite n={1} />

Medical therapy. ACC/AHA: in TAA with an average systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥130 or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥80 mm Hg, antihypertensive medications are recommended; beta blockers to achieve target BP goals are reasonable regardless of cause, and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) as an adjunct to them.<Cite n={3} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline also recommends beta blockers to achieve target BP (IIa C).<Cite n={2} /> In hypertension in patients with peripheral arterial and aortic diseases, ESC recommends an SBP target of 120–129 mm Hg if tolerated (I A), proposes ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) or ARBs as first-line (IIa B), and says statins may be considered to reduce growth and rupture of TAA (IIb B).<Cite n={1} /> According to ESC, fluoroquinolones are generally discouraged in aortic aneurysm; they may be considered if there is a compelling clinical indication and no other reasonable alternative (IIb B).<Cite n={1} />

Thresholds for elective surgery in asymptomatic patients without HTAD differ. Root and ascending aorta with a tricuspid aortic valve: ESC, ≥55 mm (I B), and in dilatation of the tubular ascending aorta with low predicted surgical risk, >52 mm (IIa B); ACC/AHA and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, ≥5.5 cm, and ≥5.0 cm when performed by experienced surgeons (in a Multidisciplinary Aortic Team according to ACC/AHA, in an expert aortic centre according to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} /> For an aneurysm of the root and ascending aorta of less than 5.5 cm that grows by ≥0.5 cm in one year or ≥0.3 cm/year in two consecutive years on tomographic imaging, ACC/AHA and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommend surgery.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> If a patient with an aneurysm of the root and ascending aorta has symptoms attributable to the aneurysm, ACC/AHA and the Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommend surgery.<Cite n={[2, 3]} />

Bicuspid aortic valve: ≥55 mm in all three guidelines; ESC, for the root phenotype, ≥50 mm (I B); the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, 5.0–5.4 cm with an additional risk factor for aortic dissection in an expert centre (IIa B).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

Arch: if symptomatic, open surgical replacement (ACC/AHA, at low or intermediate operative risk; ESC, with recurrent chest pain not attributable to other causes, I C); for an isolated asymptomatic arch aneurysm, from 55 mm (ESC IIa B and ACC/AHA, at low operative risk; Russian Ministry of Health guideline IIa C).<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

Descending thoracic aorta: ESC, ≥55 mm (I B), ACC/AHA, ≥5.5 cm; with suitable anatomy, ESC (without HTAD) and ACC/AHA (without Marfan syndrome, Loeys–Dietz syndrome or vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome) prefer thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) over open surgery.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Russian Ministry of Health guideline: TEVAR at ≥5.5 cm, open repair at ≥6.0 cm (I B).<Cite n={2} /> Degenerative TAAA: ≥6.0 cm in all three guidelines; ACC/AHA considers repair reasonable from 5.5 cm by experienced surgeons.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

## When to call an ambulance

An aneurysm without symptoms is not an emergency, but rupture or dissection of the thoracic aorta, or suspicion of them, requires emergency hospital admission.<Cite n={2} /> Without treatment, a ruptured thoracic aortic aneurysm is fatal.<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) for sudden, severe pain in the chest, back or abdomen.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> In a rupture, excruciating pain usually begins high in the back and may spread down the back and into the abdomen, and may also be felt in the chest and arms, as in a heart attack; internal bleeding quickly leads to shock.<Cite n={5} /> Coughing up blood or vomiting blood in a person with an aneurysm is also a reason to call an ambulance at once: it may be a sign that the aneurysm has broken through into the lung or the gullet.<Cite n={4} /> If a person with a known thoracic aortic aneurysm develops new symptoms or the existing ones get worse, emergency hospital admission is needed.<Cite n={2} /> Signs of dissection are described in more detail in the article on [aortic dissection](../aortic-dissection/). As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
