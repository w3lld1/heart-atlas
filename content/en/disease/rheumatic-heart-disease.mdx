Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is lasting damage to the heart, most often to its valves, that remains after one or more episodes of acute rheumatic fever (ARF).<Cite n={1} /> Rheumatic fever is not an infection itself but the body's inflammatory reaction to a streptococcal throat infection: tonsillitis or pharyngitis (strep throat).<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> RHD is life-threatening: according to WHO estimates, about 55 million people worldwide had it in 2021, and it caused about 360,000 deaths.<Cite n={1} />

Rheumatic disease most often affects the <Structure id="mitral-valve">mitral</Structure> and <Structure id="aortic-valve">aortic</Structure> valves.<Cite n={4} /> The most common late consequence is [mitral stenosis](../mitral-stenosis/), a narrowing of the mitral valve.<Cite n={4} /> This article explains where the valve damage comes from; the narrowing itself and its treatment are covered in a separate article.

## Why it happens

<Patient>

Rheumatic fever starts after tonsillitis or pharyngitis caused by group A streptococcus, usually 2 to 3 weeks later.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> Proteins of this germ resemble proteins of the body's own tissues (heart valves, heart muscle, joints), and the immune system attacks these tissues by mistake.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> Not everyone remembers the sore throat: a noticeable sore throat before rheumatic fever is recognised in only about two thirds of patients.<Cite n={4} />

The first episode of rheumatic fever is most common in children aged 5 to 14.<Cite n={1} /> An attack can come back, most often within the first year but also at any age, and repeated attacks make the valve disease worse.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> The valve disease usually begins in childhood but is most often found in people aged 20 to 39.<Cite n={1} />

The disease is linked to poverty: overcrowding, poor housing, lack of clean water and difficult access to health care.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> During the twentieth century it became much rarer in Europe and North America, but it remains common in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, Central and South Asia and the South Pacific.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ARF is a nonsuppurative, acute inflammatory complication of pharyngitis caused by group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus (GAS, *Streptococcus pyogenes*).<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> The mechanism is autoimmune: autoantibodies and T cells react against streptococcal cell wall components and, through molecular mimicry, against human antigens; GAS M proteins share epitopes with proteins of the synovium, myocardium and heart valves.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> WHO considers that superficial streptococcal skin infections may also cause ARF, whereas MSD states that only pharyngitis does; the question is under study.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

ARF develops in fewer than 1–3% of patients with untreated streptococcal pharyngitis, but in people who have had ARF a new untreated pharyngitis causes a recurrence in about 50% of cases.<Cite n={4} />

In chronic RHD the leaflets thicken, fuse and retract, and the chordae tendineae shorten, thicken and fuse; the result is valve stenosis and/or regurgitation.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> In ARF the most common cardiac manifestations are mitral regurgitation and pericarditis, sometimes aortic regurgitation; in chronic RHD they are mitral stenosis, aortic regurgitation (often with some stenosis) and sometimes tricuspid regurgitation; the tricuspid and pulmonary valves are seldom if ever affected in isolation.<Cite n={4} /> Rheumatic disease is one of the causes of [mitral regurgitation](../mitral-regurgitation/), [aortic regurgitation](../aortic-regurgitation/) and [aortic stenosis](../aortic-stenosis/), and rheumatic valve disease is among the main causes of tricuspid stenosis.<Cite n={[3, 6, 7, 8]} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Rheumatic fever most often affects the heart, large joints, the nervous system and the skin.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> Joint pain and fever usually come first.<Cite n={5} /> The ankles, knees, elbows and wrists become painful and swollen; as pain in one joint eases, another joint starts to hurt.<Cite n={5} /> The joints later recover completely.<Cite n={5} />

Inflammation of the heart can cause chest pain and a fast heartbeat, or it can go unnoticed and be found only years later, when a damaged valve is discovered.<Cite n={5} /> Some children develop rapid, jerky, uncontrollable movements of the arms, legs and face (Sydenham chorea); they usually start after the other signs and go away within several months.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> There may be a flat, painless rash with a wavy edge and small, hard, painless lumps under the skin.<Cite n={5} />

The valve disease develops slowly, over years and decades.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> Later in life, usually in middle age, it can lead to [heart failure](../heart-failure/) and [atrial fibrillation](../atrial-fibrillation/).<Cite n={5} /> How a narrowed mitral valve shows itself is described in the article on [mitral stenosis](../mitral-stenosis/).

</Patient>

<Student>

Migratory polyarthritis is the most common manifestation of ARF; the ankles, knees, elbows and wrists are usually involved, and unlike the heart, the joints are left without lasting damage ("ARF licks the joints but bites the heart").<Cite n={4} /> Carditis occurs in about 50–70% of patients in the first episode; it is considered a pancarditis (endocardium, [myocardium](../myocarditis/), [pericardium](../pericarditis/)), but valvulitis is its most consistent feature.<Cite n={4} /> Chorea often appears after the other manifestations have subsided and usually resolves completely, but recurs in about one third of patients.<Cite n={4} /> Cutaneous manifestations are uncommon and almost never occur alone.<Cite n={4} />

Prognosis after the first episode depends mostly on how severely the heart is affected and on whether ARF recurs.<Cite n={4} /> More than 85% of RHD cases are diagnosed only when complications are present.<Cite n={1} /> In pregnancy RHD is a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality; many women are unaware of their diagnosis, and pregnancy can unmask previously unrecognised valve disease.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

There is no single test that proves rheumatic fever: the doctor looks at the combination of signs using the Jones criteria.<Cite n={1} /> Doctors look for evidence of a recent streptococcal infection: by this time a throat swab often finds nothing, so blood is tested for antibodies to streptococcus.<Cite n={4} /> Blood tests show inflammation, and on an [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) the doctor looks for changes such as a prolonged PR interval.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> A heart ultrasound scan ([echocardiogram](../../diagnostic/echocardiography/)) is recommended for everyone with suspected rheumatic fever: it can find inflammation of the valves even when no murmur is heard.<Cite n={4} />

Long-term valve damage is also found with echocardiography.<Cite n={1} /> In countries where RHD is common, WHO recommends using it to check children and adolescents and suggests it may be considered for pregnant women, to find the disease early.<Cite n={1} />

</Patient>

<Student>

WHO recommends using the Jones criteria (2015 revision) to diagnose ARF in children, adolescents and adults (strong recommendation).<Cite n={1} /> With evidence of preceding GAS infection, an initial episode of ARF is diagnosed with 2 major or 1 major and 2 minor manifestations, and a recurrence also with 3 minor manifestations.<Cite n={1} /> Major criteria: carditis (clinical and/or subclinical, that is, echocardiographic valvulitis), arthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules.<Cite n={1} /> Minor criteria: polyarthralgia (monoarthralgia in moderate- and high-risk populations), fever, raised ESR and/or CRP, and a prolonged PR interval after accounting for age.<Cite n={1} /> The criteria differ between low-risk and moderate- or high-risk populations.<Cite n={1} /> Sydenham chorea alone fulfils the diagnostic criteria if other causes of movement disorder are ruled out.<Cite n={4} />

Throat cultures and rapid antigen tests are often negative by the time ARF manifests, while antistreptolysin O and anti-DNase B titres typically peak 3 to 6 weeks after pharyngitis.<Cite n={4} /> Echocardiography has become the gold standard for evaluating rheumatic carditis and valvular pathology.<Cite n={1} /> In populations with moderate or high RHD prevalence, WHO recommends echocardiographic screening of children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years (strong recommendation) and suggests that screening of pregnant women may be considered.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

The best way to prevent rheumatic fever is prompt and complete antibiotic treatment of a streptococcal sore throat.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> But most sore throats are caused by viruses, and antibiotics do not help them; the doctor decides whether an antibiotic is needed, if necessary after a test for streptococcus.<Cite n={1} />

For rheumatic fever the doctor prescribes an antibiotic to get rid of any remaining streptococci, and anti-inflammatory treatment to ease pain and inflammation.<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

After rheumatic fever and in RHD, the most important measure is secondary prevention: long-term regular antibiotic use that prevents repeat attacks and worsening of the valve disease.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> How long it continues is decided by the doctor: it often depends on how severe the disease is, and there is little evidence to guide the choice.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> It is important not to skip it: available data suggest that people who receive prophylaxis regularly have lower mortality from RHD.<Cite n={1} />

If a valve is already badly damaged, it is treated like the corresponding valve disease. In severe rheumatic mitral stenosis with a suitable valve, the fused commissures are usually separated with a balloon passed through a blood vessel (percutaneous commissurotomy); if this is not possible, the valve is operated on; a team of doctors decides.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> More about this is in the article on [mitral stenosis](../mitral-stenosis/). With rheumatic valve disease the doctor also arranges prevention of [infective endocarditis](../infective-endocarditis/) according to guidelines.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The goals of treating ARF are eradication of the streptococcal infection, relief of acute symptoms, suppression of inflammation and prophylaxis against future infection.<Cite n={4} /> It is unknown whether adding anti-inflammatory agents to antibiotics affects progression to RHD or the severity of carditis.<Cite n={1} />

WHO recommends antibiotics for sore throat with a positive test for GAS, antibiotic prophylaxis against recurrence for all patients with ARF or RHD, and support for treatment adherence (all strong recommendations); there is limited evidence to guide the duration of prophylaxis.<Cite n={1} /> The Russian Ministry of Health recommends antibacterial prophylaxis against recurrence of rheumatic fever in patients with rheumatic mitral stenosis.<Cite n={2} /> Drugs, intervals and duration are set by the doctor according to current guidelines.

In severe rheumatic mitral stenosis with favourable anatomy, percutaneous mitral commissurotomy (PMC) is the standard of care; absence of commissural fusion is a contraindication to it, and with unfavourable anatomy valve surgery is performed.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> Tertiary prevention means treating the complications of RHD, including with anticoagulants and cardiac interventions.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

Rheumatic valve disease can suddenly get worse: when mitral stenosis decompensates, fluid builds up in the lungs (pulmonary oedema), and pulmonary oedema and an attack of atrial fibrillation are reasons for emergency hospital admission.<Cite n={2} /> So if severe breathlessness or a feeling of suffocation comes on suddenly, especially when lying flat, or an attack of fast, irregular heartbeat begins, call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK). The complications of the valve disease are described in the article on [mitral stenosis](../mitral-stenosis/).

If there are signs of a stroke (sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, a drooping face), call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away. As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
