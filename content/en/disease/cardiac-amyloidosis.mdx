Cardiac amyloidosis (amyloid cardiomyopathy) is a disease in which amyloid — a protein with a particular fibrillar structure — is deposited in the tissues of the heart.<Cite n={3} /> Amyloid forms from misfolded proteins: they clump together into insoluble fibres that are deposited in organs outside the cells and physically interfere with how the organ works.<Cite n={4} /> The two types that most often affect the heart are AL and ATTR amyloidosis.<Cite n={2} /> Involvement of the heart is the most dangerous form of amyloidosis.<Cite n={6} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

Proteins in the body fold into a particular shape. In amyloidosis a protein folds abnormally, and these proteins clump together and build up in the tissues. The body cannot easily break these deposits down, and they interfere with how the organ works.<Cite n={6} />

In AL amyloidosis, plasma cells — immune cells that make antibodies — produce excessive amounts of abnormal proteins called light chains. Some people with AL amyloidosis also have a cancer of the plasma cells, multiple myeloma.<Cite n={6} />

In ATTR amyloidosis the deposited protein is transthyretin, which is made by the liver.<Cite n={6} /> ATTR amyloidosis can be hereditary, when the transthyretin gene is altered, or "wild-type", when ordinary, unaltered protein is deposited.<Cite n={4} /> Wild-type amyloidosis usually affects the heart and is much more common in men than in women.<Cite n={6} />

Amyloid infiltrates the heart muscle, which becomes stiff; the heart fills with blood less well, and over time [heart failure](../heart-failure/) develops. Heart rhythm problems and heart block can occur, and blood pressure is often low.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Amyloidosis is a group of disparate conditions characterised by extracellular deposition of insoluble fibrils composed of misaggregated proteins.<Cite n={4} /> The deposits themselves are metabolically inert but interfere physically with organ structure and function.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline defines amyloid cardiomyopathy as a form of systemic disorder of protein metabolism with predominant deposition of amyloid in the tissues of the heart (myocardium, endocardium, pericardium), the aorta and the walls of the coronary vessels.<Cite n={3} />

The main systemic types: AL — overproduction of an amyloidogenic immunoglobulin light chain in a monoclonal plasma cell or other B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder; ATTRv (hereditary) — inheritance of a mutant gene encoding a protein prone to misfolding, most commonly transthyretin (TTR); ATTRwt — aggregation and deposition of wild-type TTR, principally targeting the heart.<Cite n={4} />

Transthyretin is a tetramer of four identical subunits that transports thyroxine and retinol. Pathogenic variants make the tetramer thermodynamically unstable; it dissociates into oligomers and monomers, from which amyloid fibrils form.<Cite n={3} /> Depending on the gene variant, cardiomyopathy or polyneuropathy predominates, but both can occur together, and symptoms vary even within one family.<Cite n={3} />

Infiltration of the myocardium causes a restrictive cardiomyopathy with diastolic dysfunction and heart failure; AV block and arrhythmias may occur, and hypotension is common.<Cite n={4} /> The stiff, noncompliant LV fills poorly in diastole and filling pressures rise; stroke volume is relatively fixed, so cardiac output depends on heart rate.<Cite n={5} /> Chronically raised filling pressures enlarge both atria and predispose to atrial arrhythmias; in amyloidosis, intracardiac thrombi may form, with a risk of systemic embolism.<Cite n={5} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

Common general signs of amyloidosis are tiredness and weight loss. When the heart is affected, heart rhythm problems and heart failure occur and cause breathlessness, weakness or fainting.<Cite n={6} /> As in other stiff (restrictive) cardiomyopathies, the person tolerates exercise less well, gets breathless on walking and when lying flat, and notices palpitations, a swollen abdomen and swollen legs.<Cite n={5} />

None of these complaints is specific, so the diagnosis is often made late. A clue is that the disease gradually affects several organs at once.<Cite n={4} /> If the nerves are affected, there may be tingling and numbness in the fingers and toes and dizziness on standing up.<Cite n={6} />

Important clues can also appear outside the heart. Carpal tunnel syndrome in both hands, a ruptured biceps tendon, narrowing of the spinal canal in the lower back and hearing loss are typical of ATTR amyloidosis; bruises that appear easily and an enlarged tongue are typical of AL amyloidosis.<Cite n={2} /> In wild-type ATTR amyloidosis, carpal tunnel syndrome, tendon rupture and narrowing of the spinal canal may appear years before the heart is affected.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The ESC 2026 guideline lists "red flags" for the most common forms of cardiac amyloidosis. Extracardiac: polyneuropathy and dysautonomia (TTR and AL), skin bruising and macroglossia (AL), deafness, bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, ruptured biceps tendon, lumbar spinal stenosis, vitreous deposits, family history (TTR), kidney insufficiency and proteinuria (TTR and AL).<Cite n={2} />

Cardiac and laboratory (both forms): hypotension, or normotension in a previously hypertensive patient; pseudo-infarct ECG pattern; low or decreased QRS voltage relative to LV thickness; AV conduction disease; NT-proBNP disproportionately elevated relative to HF severity; persistently elevated cardiac troponin.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

To assess heart involvement, doctors record an [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) and take blood tests for NT-proBNP and troponin.<Cite n={4} /> A typical clue is a mismatch: on [echocardiography](../../diagnostic/echocardiography/) the heart walls are thick, but on the ECG the electrical signals are small.<Cite n={3} /> A contrast-enhanced cardiac MRI is often done as well: the European guidelines recommend it for everyone with a cardiomyopathy at the first assessment.<Cite n={1} />

If amyloidosis is suspected, the European guidelines recommend two tests at once: blood and urine tests for an abnormal (monoclonal) protein, and scintigraphy — a scan of the heart after injection of a radioactive tracer that is also used to image bones.<Cite n={2} /> If the tests for monoclonal protein are normal and the tracer clearly builds up in the heart, ATTR amyloidosis can be diagnosed without a heart biopsy.<Cite n={[1, 4]} /> If the tests are abnormal, AL amyloidosis is suspected: a haematologist is needed, and the diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy.<Cite n={[1, 2]} />

It is important to identify the type of amyloid precisely, because AL and ATTR are treated differently.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> In ATTR amyloidosis a genetic test is done to find out whether the form is hereditary.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> For hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis, the Russian Ministry of Health guideline recommends a consultation with a geneticist both for the patient and for family members.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Screening according to ESC 2023: LV wall thickness ≥12 mm plus at least one feature, for example heart failure or aortic stenosis at age ≥65 years, hypotension (or normotension if previously hypertensive), polyneuropathy or autonomic dysfunction, proteinuria, skin bruising, ruptured biceps tendon, bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, subendocardial or transmural late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) or increased ECV on CMR, reduced longitudinal strain with apical sparing, decreased QRS voltage to mass ratio, pseudo-Q waves and AV conduction disease on the ECG, possible family history of ATTR, chronically increased troponin, known multiple myeloma or MGUS.<Cite n={1} />

ECG: a mismatch between low voltage and myocardial mass, pseudo-infarct changes, rhythm disturbances (most often atrial fibrillation) and conduction disease.<Cite n={3} /> In the [hypertrophic cardiomyopathy](../hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy/) phenotype, ESC 2023 lists AV block, low QRS voltage and Q waves/pseudoinfarction pattern among the findings that should raise the possibility of amyloidosis.<Cite n={1} />

Echocardiography: marked unexplained biventricular wall thickening, LV wall >12 mm in the absence of hypertension, HCM, "athlete's heart" and other causes, moderate pericardial effusion, valve thickening, apical sparing, left atrial dilatation; LV contractility may be preserved or reduced.<Cite n={3} /> ESC 2026 adds granular sparkling of the myocardium and increased right ventricular wall and interatrial septum thickness.<Cite n={2} />

ESC 2023 recommends contrast-enhanced CMR for all patients with cardiomyopathy at initial evaluation (I B).<Cite n={1} /> CMR features of amyloidosis: global subendocardial or transmural LGE, elevated native T1 and increased extracellular volume (ECV).<Cite n={2} />

ESC 2026 (I B): in patients with HF and suspected cardiac amyloidosis, initial diagnostic testing with serum and urine immunofixation, serum free light chain assay and DPD/PYP/HMDP bone scintigraphy.<Cite n={2} /> In the ESC 2023 algorithm, grade 2–3 uptake with normal haematological tests establishes cardiac ATTR amyloidosis, followed by TTR genetic testing (ATTRwt or ATTRv); grade 1 uptake requires histological confirmation; abnormal haematological tests with grade 1–3 uptake require histological confirmation (usually cardiac) to subtype.<Cite n={1} /> In the ESC 2026 algorithm, grade 1 uptake calls for an individualised approach: CMR, genetic testing, specific tests for rare forms, biopsy or repeat scintigraphy in 6–12 months.<Cite n={2} />

Amyloidosis is confirmed histologically by demonstrating fibrillar deposits in an involved organ; if a fat aspirate is negative, a clinically involved organ is biopsied, and sections are stained with Congo red.<Cite n={4} /> Accurate typing is essential so that patients with ATTRwt are not given the chemotherapy used for AL amyloidosis.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

Treatment depends on the type of amyloidosis.<Cite n={4} /> A person with cardiac amyloidosis is looked after by a team: a cardiologist, a neurologist, a nephrologist and, in AL amyloidosis, a haematologist.<Cite n={1} />

In ATTR amyloidosis, transthyretin stabilisers — medicines that stop the protein from breaking apart and forming fibres — and medicines that reduce the production of transthyretin are used.<Cite n={4} /> The 2026 European guidelines recommend one of these treatments for ATTR cardiac amyloidosis when heart failure symptoms have not reached the most severe level (functional classes I–III): it slows the worsening of symptoms and lowers the risk of hospital admission and death.<Cite n={2} />

In AL amyloidosis, treatment targets the abnormal plasma cells: this is chemotherapy directed by a haematologist.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> Starting it quickly is important to preserve organ function.<Cite n={4} />

If the body retains fluid (swelling, breathlessness), the main task is to treat this congestion: diuretics are used, and salt and fluid are restricted.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> Diuretics have to be adjusted carefully: if too much fluid is removed, the heart pumps out less blood and blood pressure falls.<Cite n={5} /> Some of the usual heart failure medicines are poorly tolerated in amyloidosis, and some are contraindicated.<Cite n={3} /> So do not start or change any medicines without your doctor.

In atrial fibrillation, unless there are contraindications, medicines that reduce blood clotting are prescribed to lower the risk of stroke.<Cite n={1} /> A very slow pulse and severe heart block may require a pacemaker.<Cite n={5} /> Heart transplantation has been successful in carefully selected patients with severe heart involvement.<Cite n={4} /> The doctor and the patient decide together which path to take. The outlook depends on the type of amyloidosis and on which organs are affected; involvement of the heart is the most dangerous form of the disease.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> That is why it matters to make the diagnosis in time, identify the type of amyloid precisely and start the treatment that suits that type.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

ESC 2026 (I A): treatment with a TTR silencer or a TTR stabiliser is recommended for patients with variant or wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis and NYHA class I–III to reduce progression of symptoms and the risk of CV hospitalisation and all-cause death.<Cite n={2} /> Stabilisers stabilise circulating TTR tetramers, inhibiting TTR misfolding and fibril formation; TTR gene silencing with antisense RNA or RNA interference blocks translation of TTR mRNA and reduces serum TTR levels.<Cite n={4} /> In AL amyloidosis, prompt initiation of anti-plasma cell therapy is essential to preserve organ function and prolong life.<Cite n={4} />

Supportive treatment: patients with amyloid cardiomyopathy need an individual approach because of the risk of orthostatic hypotension and the limited tolerance of a number of standard heart failure regimens; with congestion, the priority is controlling fluid retention and volume overload.<Cite n={3} /> Patients with restrictive cardiomyopathy are highly preload dependent, so excessive diuresis may reduce cardiac output and cause hypotension.<Cite n={5} />

Oral anticoagulation is recommended in all patients with cardiac amyloidosis and atrial fibrillation or flutter, unless contraindicated (I B).<Cite n={1} /> Anticoagulation is also indicated with a documented intracardiac thrombus; bradyarrhythmias and advanced conduction system disease may necessitate permanent pacemaker implantation.<Cite n={5} /> Heart transplantation has been successful in carefully selected patients with AL and both types of ATTR amyloidosis and severe cardiac involvement.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

For hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis, the Russian Ministry of Health guideline names acute impairment of heart function as a reason for emergency hospital admission.<Cite n={3} /> Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if breathlessness suddenly gets much worse, especially at rest or when lying flat, or if you faint: heart involvement can cause heart failure, rhythm problems and heart block.<Cite n={[4, 6]} />

If there are signs of a stroke (sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, a drooping face), call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away. As with any heart condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
