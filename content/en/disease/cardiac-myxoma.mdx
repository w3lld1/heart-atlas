A myxoma is a benign (non-cancerous) tumour of the heart, usually irregular in shape and jelly-like in consistency.<Cite n={[1, 5]} /> It is the most common primary cardiac tumour, that is, a tumour that arises in the heart itself rather than spreading to it from other organs.<Cite n={[4, 6]} /> Primary cardiac tumours are rare; tumours that have spread to the heart from elsewhere are much more common.<Cite n={4} /> A myxoma most often grows in the <Structure id="left-atrium">left atrium</Structure>.<Cite n={[4, 5, 6]} /> Although the tumour is benign, it can obstruct blood flow through the heart, and pieces that break off it can block arteries, for example an artery in the brain, causing a stroke.<Cite n={[2, 5]} />

## Why it happens

<Patient>

Myxomas develop in women more often than in men.<Cite n={5} /> Some uncommon myxomas run in families: they are part of Carney complex, a syndrome of various non-cancerous tumours, and can grow in any chamber of the heart, in one or in several.<Cite n={5} />

A myxoma in the left atrium often grows from a stalk and swings freely with the flow of blood, like a ball on a string. As it swings, it may move in and out of the nearby <Structure id="mitral-valve">mitral valve</Structure>, over and over again blocking and unblocking the way for blood from the left atrium into the left ventricle.<Cite n={5} /> Pieces of the tumour or blood clots that form on its surface may break off, travel in the bloodstream to other organs and block arteries there.<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, myxoma accounts for 45 to 85% of all primary cardiac tumours, and its incidence in females is 2 to 3 times that in males.<Cite n={4} /> According to the abstract of the StatPearls chapter, more than 75% of myxomas originate in the left atrium, either at the mitral annulus or at the fossa ovalis border of the interatrial septum; 20% arise from the right atrium and 5% from both atria.<Cite n={6} /> The ESC figure on the location of cardiac tumours lists myxoma among tumours of both the left and the right atrium.<Cite n={1} />

Most myxomas are pedunculated; the rest are broad-based and sessile; they may be up to 15 cm in diameter. They may be myxoid and gelatinous; smooth, firm and lobular; or friable and irregular; friable, irregular myxomas increase the risk of systemic embolism.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline on ischaemic stroke lists left atrial myxoma among sources of high cardioembolic risk.<Cite n={2} />

Carney complex is a familial, autosomal dominant syndrome of recurrent cardiac myxomas combined with cutaneous myxomas, pigmented skin lesions, endocrine tumours and other neoplasms. Patients are often young at presentation, may have multiple myxomas (particularly in the ventricles) and have a higher risk of recurrence.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

A person with a myxoma in the left atrium may feel short of breath or faint when standing: gravity pulls the tumour into the opening of the mitral valve, the heart pumps out less blood and blood pressure drops for a while. Lying down usually moves the tumour away from the valve and relieves the symptoms.<Cite n={5} />

There may also be other symptoms: fever, weight loss, joint pain and Raynaud syndrome, in which the fingers and toes become cold and painful in the cold.<Cite n={5} />

The most common complication is embolism. What happens depends on which artery is blocked: for example, an embolus from a left atrial myxoma in an artery of the brain may cause a stroke, and an embolus from a right atrial myxoma in an artery of the lung may cause pain and coughing up of blood.<Cite n={5} /> A low red blood cell count (anaemia) can cause tiredness, weakness and paleness, and a low platelet count can cause red, purple or brown spots on the skin and bruises.<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Myxomas may manifest with the triad of heart failure, embolic disease and constitutional symptoms.<Cite n={4} />

A myxoma may cause a diastolic murmur that mimics the murmur of [mitral stenosis](../mitral-stenosis/) but whose loudness and location vary from beat to beat with body position; about 15% of pedunculated left atrial myxomas produce an audible "tumour plop" as they drop into the mitral orifice during diastole.<Cite n={4} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline lists left atrial myxoma among the causes of acute [mitral regurgitation](../mitral-regurgitation/) related to the leaflets.<Cite n={3} />

Myxomas may also cause arrhythmias; Raynaud phenomenon and finger clubbing are less typical but may occur.<Cite n={4} /> Thrombi and tumour fragments, especially from gelatinous myxomas, may embolise to the brain, coronary arteries, kidneys, spleen, limbs or lungs.<Cite n={4} /> Symptoms are often typical of much more common disorders such as [heart failure](../heart-failure/), stroke and coronary artery disease, and extracardiac findings may suggest bacterial [endocarditis](../infective-endocarditis/), systemic rheumatic disorders or other cancers.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A myxoma is suspected from the symptoms; when listening to the heart, the doctor may hear a murmur.<Cite n={5} /> The same symptoms occur in many other conditions, so extensive testing may be needed before a diagnosis is made, and it is often delayed.<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

The first test is a [heart ultrasound (echocardiography)](../../diagnostic/echocardiography/), often followed by a heart MRI or other imaging such as CT to tell a myxoma from other types of tumour.<Cite n={5} /> Blood tests may show a high white blood cell count, anaemia and a low platelet count, but none of these tests is conclusive.<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

In the ESC algorithm for cardiac masses, non-invasive assessment includes echocardiography, CT, cardiac magnetic resonance and PET; cardiac mass biopsy is considered if a malignant primary tumour is suspected or the diagnosis is uncertain on imaging.<Cite n={1} /> Transoesophageal echocardiography is better for visualising atrial tumours, and transthoracic echocardiography for ventricular tumours.<Cite n={4} /> According to the Russian Ministry of Health guideline, standard transthoracic echocardiography in older patients with ischaemic stroke or TIA can identify causes that include tumours in the heart chambers.<Cite n={2} />

Cardiac MRI is the imaging modality of choice for tumour tissue characterisation.<Cite n={4} /> The typical appearance of a myxoma on CT angiography is a left atrial mass attached by a stalk to the interatrial septum.<Cite n={4} /> Biopsy is not usually performed, because imaging can often distinguish benign from malignant tumours.<Cite n={4} />

Anaemia, thrombocytopenia and raised white cell count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein and gamma-globulins are common; the [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) may show left atrial enlargement.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

A myxoma is removed surgically.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> The operation usually cures the person.<Cite n={5} /> After surgery, echocardiography is done from time to time to check that the myxoma has not come back.<Cite n={5} /> Whether and when an operation is needed is decided by the doctors together with the patient.

</Patient>

<Student>

According to the ESC table on benign cardiac tumours, surgical resection is indicated in symptomatic patients; non-surgical management applies, in particular, to rhabdomyomas, intramural haemangioma and some unresectable tumours.<Cite n={1} /> For an asymptomatic tumour, a multidisciplinary team (MDT) decides, considering tumour type, location, size, growth rate and likelihood of embolism; if the tumour is left-sided and endocardial, the MDT considers removal because of the embolic risk, even if the mass is small and incidental.<Cite n={1} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline on ischaemic stroke recommends resection of an atrial myxoma found in children with ischaemic stroke, to reduce the risk of tumour-related cardioembolism (grade of recommendation C, level of evidence 5).<Cite n={2} />

After excision of a benign tumour, echocardiography or other imaging is repeated to monitor for recurrence; in Carney complex the risk of myxoma recurrence is higher.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

The most common complication of a myxoma is embolism: an embolus from a left atrial myxoma can block an artery in the brain and cause a stroke.<Cite n={[2, 5]} /> The Russian Ministry of Health guideline lists the signs of a stroke as sudden weakness of the muscles of the face, an arm or a leg, especially on one side of the body, altered sensation, and difficulty speaking or understanding speech; in severe cases the person loses consciousness.<Cite n={2} /> To check, ask the person to smile, to speak and to raise both arms: if they find any of these hard to do, call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) at once and say when the symptoms began.<Cite n={2} /> Until the ambulance arrives, the person is laid down comfortably with the head and shoulders resting on a raised support so that there is no bending, tight clothing is loosened, enough fresh air is let in, and nothing is given by mouth.<Cite n={2} />

With a left atrial myxoma, breathlessness and fainting occur when the tumour blocks the opening of the mitral valve, and an embolus from a right atrial myxoma in an artery of the lung may cause pain and coughing up of blood.<Cite n={5} /> As with any heart condition, call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) for fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page.
