Raynaud phenomenon is a condition in which, during attacks, the small arteries of the fingers suddenly narrow (spasm), usually in response to cold or emotional stress, and the fingers change colour: they usually turn white, then blue, then red.<Cite n={[1, 2, 4]} /> The fingers and toes are affected most often, and occasionally other protruding parts of the body, such as the nose or the tongue.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> The changes are reversible: they go away once the trigger stops acting, and warming the hands speeds up the return of normal colour and sensation.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health's catalogue of clinical guidelines has no separate guideline on Raynaud phenomenon: it is described in the guidelines on vibration disease, systemic sclerosis in children and acute limb ischaemia, so much of the information in this article comes from the MSD Manuals.

## Why it happens

<Patient>

According to the MSD Manuals, the mechanism is not fully understood: the blood vessels of the fingers probably respond too strongly to signals from the sympathetic nervous system, which narrows the arteries in response to cold and strong emotions.<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

Raynaud phenomenon can be primary, when there is no other disease (it is also called Raynaud disease), or secondary, when it is caused by another disease.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> According to MSD, Raynaud phenomenon affects about 3–5% of people, more often women and younger people, and in 80–90% of cases it is primary.<Cite n={4} />

According to MSD, secondary Raynaud phenomenon most often accompanies systemic rheumatic diseases: systemic sclerosis (scleroderma), systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis and others.<Cite n={4} /> Other causes MSD lists include an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), carpal tunnel syndrome, frostbite, vibration, and some medicines and substances, including nicotine.<Cite n={4} /> Nicotine commonly contributes to secondary Raynaud phenomenon, but this is often overlooked.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline describes Raynaud syndrome ("white finger syndrome") as one of the manifestations of vibration disease, an occupational disease: these are attacks in which the fingers turn white, starting at the fingertips, with a clear border.<Cite n={1} /> This form is typical of exposure to high- and medium-frequency local vibration.<Cite n={1} />

According to MSD, Raynaud phenomenon sometimes occurs together with other conditions in which the arteries are prone to spasm: migraine, variant angina (chest pain at rest) and [pulmonary hypertension](../pulmonary-hypertension/).<Cite n={5} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline on systemic sclerosis in children defines Raynaud phenomenon as episodes of transient digital ischaemia caused by vasoconstriction of the digital arteries, precapillary arterioles and cutaneous arteriovenous shunts in response to cold and emotional stress.<Cite n={2} /> According to MSD, the probable cause is an exaggerated alpha-2 adrenergic response that triggers vasospasm.<Cite n={4} />

According to the same guideline, Raynaud syndrome develops in 75–78% of patients with systemic sclerosis (in children it may be subtle; in adults it is an obligatory feature of the disease) and is often the first manifestation, preceding involvement of other organs by many months or even years.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## Symptoms

<Patient>

An attack begins quickly, most often in the cold, and lasts minutes or hours.<Cite n={5} /> The fingers become pale, white or bluish, usually in patches; only one finger or part of it may be affected.<Cite n={5} /> Numbness, tingling, a pins-and-needles sensation and burning are common.<Cite n={5} /> As the attack ends, the skin may be redder, bluer or darker than usual; warming restores normal colour and sensation.<Cite n={5} />

According to MSD, in primary Raynaud phenomenon the spasm is rarely severe enough for tissue to die.<Cite n={4} /> If attacks keep recurring and last a long time, especially in systemic sclerosis, the skin of the fingers may become smooth, shiny and tight, and small painful sores may appear on the fingertips.<Cite n={5} /> Secondary Raynaud phenomenon due to a systemic rheumatic disease may lead to painful gangrene of the fingers.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, the colour changes may be triphasic (pallor, then cyanosis and, after warming, erythema due to reactive hyperaemia), biphasic (cyanosis, erythema) or uniphasic (pallor only or cyanosis only).<Cite n={4} />

The changes are often symmetrical and do not extend proximal to the metacarpophalangeal joints; the middle three fingers are affected most often, and the thumb rarely.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is diagnosed

<Patient>

A doctor usually suspects Raynaud phenomenon from the description of the attacks and an examination, and often no special tests are needed to make the diagnosis.<Cite n={5} /> The main aim of the assessment is to work out whether the Raynaud phenomenon is primary or secondary.<Cite n={4} /> For this, blood tests for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases may be ordered, and if the doctor suspects a blocked artery, a Doppler ultrasound before and after cold exposure.<Cite n={[4, 5]} />

According to MSD, findings that point to primary Raynaud phenomenon are onset before the age of 40, mild symmetrical attacks in both hands, no tissue death and no signs of another disease.<Cite n={4} /> Findings that point to secondary Raynaud phenomenon are onset after 40, severe painful attacks, sometimes in one hand only, ulcers and other signs of reduced blood supply to tissues, and signs of an associated disease.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

According to MSD, the diagnostic criteria are based on a history of unusual cold sensitivity, biphasic colour changes of the digits (white and blue digits) and additional findings from the history and examination.<Cite n={4} /> A negative or low (less than 1:40) antinuclear antibody (ANA) titre also favours primary Raynaud phenomenon.<Cite n={4} />

The Russian Ministry of Health guideline on systemic sclerosis in children recommends nailfold capillaroscopy for all patients; Raynaud syndrome and typical capillaroscopy changes are part of the preliminary criteria for juvenile systemic sclerosis.<Cite n={2} /> In vibration disease, a cold test is used to confirm Raynaud syndrome: a provocation test in which the hands are immersed in pre-cooled water.<Cite n={1} />

Acrocyanosis also causes colour changes of the digits in the cold, but unlike Raynaud phenomenon it is persistent, not easily reversed and does not cause trophic changes, ulcers or pain.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## How it is treated

<Patient>

For primary Raynaud phenomenon, MSD describes these measures: avoiding cold, stopping smoking and, if stress triggers the attacks, learning relaxation techniques or getting counselling.<Cite n={4} /> It is not only the hands that need protecting from cold: according to MSD, mild Raynaud phenomenon can be controlled by protecting the head, trunk, arms and legs from cold.<Cite n={5} /> Smoking must be avoided: nicotine narrows the blood vessels.<Cite n={5} />

In addition to these measures, a doctor may prescribe medicines.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> In secondary Raynaud phenomenon, the main thing is to treat the underlying disease.<Cite n={[4, 5]} /> Whether medicines are needed, and which ones, is for the doctor to decide; do not start, stop or change medicines on your own.

</Patient>

<Student>

MSD describes cervical or local sympathectomy as controversial: it is reserved for patients with progressive disability in whom all other measures, including treatment of the underlying disease, have failed.<Cite n={4} /> Sympathectomy often abolishes the symptoms, but relief may be temporary.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

A typical attack of Raynaud phenomenon is reversible and goes away once the trigger stops acting.<Cite n={4} /> However, the Russian Ministry of Health guideline lists Raynaud disease among the less common causes of acute ischaemia of the arm: a sudden disruption of blood flow to a limb that needs urgent diagnosis and treatment.<Cite n={3} /> A typical Raynaud attack does not spread above the base of the fingers and goes away after warming.<Cite n={4} /> Call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) straight away if an arm or a leg suddenly becomes very painful, pale, mottled or bluish, cold, numb or tingling, or weak or paralysed<Cite n={3} />, especially if the changes have spread above the fingers or do not go away after warming. Read more in the article on [acute limb ischaemia](../acute-limb-ischemia/).

According to MSD, sores on the fingertips, severe painful attacks, attacks in one hand only and onset after the age of 40 point to secondary Raynaud phenomenon; in that case the doctor looks for and treats the underlying disease.<Cite n={4} />

As with any heart or blood vessel condition, call an ambulance for fainting, chest pain or severe shortness of breath: this is the site's general safety rule, repeated at the bottom of every page. Another disease of the limb arteries is covered in the article on [peripheral artery disease of the legs](../peripheral-artery-disease/).
