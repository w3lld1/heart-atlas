Ambulatory ECG monitoring is a recording of the heart's electrical activity made by a portable device over a long period while the person goes about their everyday life.<Cite n={4} /> A standard [ECG](../ecg/) gives only a snapshot of how the heart is working and can miss problems that come and go.<Cite n={4} /> The best-known device of this kind is the Holter monitor, but there are also event recorders, patch monitors and implantable loop recorders.<Cite n={4} />

  <p slot="recording">Holter monitor: electrodes on the chest are connected to a small device worn on the belt or carried in a pouch. It records the ECG without a break, usually for 24 to 72 hours, while the person goes about their everyday life.<Cite n={4} /></p>
  <p slot="symptom">When symptoms appear, the person presses the button on the device or writes the time in a diary.<Cite n={4} /> Later the doctor looks at what the heart rhythm was doing at exactly that minute: a match between symptoms and the recording is the main thing monitoring looks for.<Cite n={2} /></p>
</Diagram>

## What it shows

<Patient>

The recording shows what the heart rhythm was like for the whole monitoring period, including the moments when the person had symptoms.<Cite n={4} /> The main thing the doctor looks for is a match: whether the symptoms happened at a time when the recording shows a rhythm problem.<Cite n={2} /> If there was no rhythm problem on the recording during a faint, that is also an important result: it does not give a diagnosis yet, but it allows a rhythm problem to be ruled out as the cause of the faint.<Cite n={2} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The ESC describes a correlation between symptoms and a documented arrhythmia as the gold standard for the diagnosis of arrhythmic syncope.<Cite n={2} /> Asymptomatic significant arrhythmias have also been considered by several authors to be a diagnostic finding: prolonged asystole (≥3 s), rapid supraventricular tachycardias (>160 b.p.m. for >32 beats) and ventricular tachycardias.<Cite n={2} /> Holter recordings can be acquired in 2-, 3- or 12-channel formats.<Cite n={4} /> According to the StatPearls review, 24-hour ambulatory monitoring helps identify and evaluate early ischaemic heart disease, but dynamic ST-segment changes may be confounded by body position or cardiac medications, so cautious interpretation is warranted.<Cite n={4} /> In [atrial fibrillation](../../disease/atrial-fibrillation/), the ESC includes ambulatory ECG monitoring in the work-up to assess AF burden and ventricular rate control.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## When it is done

<Patient>

Monitoring is usually arranged for faints and near-faints without a clear cause, and for palpitations.<Cite n={4} /> Another reason is a stroke whose cause could not be found (a cryptogenic stroke): a longer recording finds atrial fibrillation more often in these patients.<Cite n={4} />

Which device is chosen depends above all on how often the episodes happen.<Cite n={1} /> If they happen every day or several times a week, a Holter monitor is suitable; for rare episodes, devices that record for weeks or even years are needed.<Cite n={[1, 4]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

For people with a suspected cardiac arrhythmic cause of syncope, NICE (CG109) recommends an ambulatory ECG and chooses its type according to the frequency of transient loss of consciousness (TLoC):<Cite n={1} />

- TLoC at least several times a week: Holter monitoring (up to 48 hours if necessary); if no further TLoC occurs during the monitoring period, an external event recorder that provides continuous recording with the facility for the patient to indicate when a symptomatic event has occurred;<Cite n={1} />
- TLoC every 1 to 2 weeks: an external event recorder; if TLoC occurs outside the period of external event recording, an implantable event recorder;<Cite n={1} />
- TLoC less than once every 2 weeks: an implantable event recorder; a Holter monitor should not usually be offered unless there is evidence of a conduction abnormality on the 12-lead ECG.<Cite n={1} />

NICE also advises that in unexplained syncope the ambulatory ECG should come before a tilt test, and that an implantable event recorder should have both patient-activated and automatic detection modes.<Cite n={1} />

The ESC sets out a general principle: as a rule, ECG monitoring is indicated only when there is a high pre-test probability of identifying an arrhythmia.<Cite n={2} /> Holter monitoring may be of more value if symptoms are frequent, and external loop recorders can be useful in patients with relatively frequent syncope episodes.<Cite n={2} /> The ESC notes that the indications for implantable loop recorders have expanded: they are now used in an early phase of evaluation in patients with recurrent syncope of uncertain origin, in the absence of high-risk criteria and structural heart disease.<Cite n={2} />

</Student>

## How to prepare and how it is done

<Patient>

The clinic will explain how to prepare for the recording: follow the instructions of your doctor or nurse.

**Holter monitor.** Electrodes are stuck to the chest and connected to a small device worn on the belt or carried in a pouch.<Cite n={4} /> It records continuously, usually for 24 to 72 hours.<Cite n={4} /> The person writes their symptoms in a diary or marks them by pressing a button on the device.<Cite n={4} /> It is important that the person is given a thorough explanation of how to handle the device before the recording starts.<Cite n={4} />

An **external loop recorder** continuously records the ECG into a "loop" memory and, when the button is pressed, saves a stretch of recording from before and after the press.<Cite n={4} /> A **patch monitor** is a device with an adhesive backing that is usually worn for about 14 days; most of them do not get in the way of everyday life.<Cite n={4} />

An **implantable loop recorder** is placed under the skin of the chest wall during a minimally invasive procedure without general anaesthesia, and it can record data for up to 3 years.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Traditional post-event recorders are small devices that record a single ECG lead for several minutes.<Cite n={4} /> Some monitors feature algorithms that can detect irregularities, particularly atrial fibrillation.<Cite n={4} /> NICE advises instructing the person and their family and/or carer how to operate an implantable event recorder, and prompt follow-up (data interrogation of the device) after any further episode of TLoC.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## What the results mean

<Patient>

Interpreting a long recording takes expertise and time, so it is read by a specialist.<Cite n={4} /> The doctor compares any rhythm changes found with the symptom diary and assesses them together with the examination, the ECG and other tests.<Cite n={[2, 4]} /> A normal recording does not rule out heart disease. If the episodes are rare, they may simply not have happened during the monitoring period.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The duration of recording strongly affects the result: in randomised trials in patients with cryptogenic stroke, prolonged monitoring significantly increased the detection of atrial fibrillation.<Cite n={4} /> If no TLoC occurs during Holter monitoring, NICE suggests an external event recorder as the next step.<Cite n={1} />

</Student>

## Limitations

<Patient>

A Holter monitor records for only one to three days, and if no episode happens during that time, it will not show the cause.<Cite n={4} /> Discomfort and skin irritation from the electrodes and the adhesive are common reasons why the device cannot be worn for the whole prescribed time.<Cite n={4} /> A Holter monitor does not send data in real time, so an important event may only be seen after the recording has ended.<Cite n={4} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The ESC names the principal limitation of any ECG monitoring device as the inability to record blood pressure together with the ECG.<Cite n={2} /> The ESC states that the true yield of Holter monitoring in syncope may be as low as 1–2% in an unselected population; external loop recorders generally have a higher diagnostic yield.<Cite n={2} /> The utility of Holter monitors is limited by patient compliance and the brevity of the monitoring period.<Cite n={4} /> Patch recorders may have reduced signal quality in certain patients.<Cite n={4} />

</Student>

## When to call an ambulance

Monitoring is a planned test and does not replace help in an emergency. If you have chest pain, faint or have a strong, pounding heartbeat, call your local emergency number (112 in the EU, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) without waiting for the test or for the recording to finish. Do the same if you have severe shortness of breath: this is a general rule of the site and is repeated at the bottom of every page.
