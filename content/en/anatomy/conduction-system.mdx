Every heartbeat is set off by an electrical signal, and that signal starts within the heart itself.<Cite n={1} /> It arises in the sinoatrial (sinus) node in the right atrium and always follows the same route: the atrioventricular (AV) node, the bundle of His, the right and left bundle branches, and the Purkinje fibres.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> This route is called the cardiac conduction system. Thanks to it, the atria contract first and the ventricles afterwards.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

## How it is built

<Patient>

The **sinoatrial node** (sinus node) is a cluster of specialised cells in the upper part of the right atrium.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> These cells produce electrical impulses on their own, without any outside signal, which is why the sinus node is called the heart's pacemaker: normally it sets the heart rate.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

The **AV node** (atrioventricular, meaning between the atria and the ventricles) lies in the lower part of the right atrium, next to the wall between the chambers.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> It is the only "gateway" for the impulse from the atria to the ventricles: everywhere else they are separated by connective tissue, which does not let the impulse through.<Cite n={3} />

The **bundle of His** runs from the AV node down the wall between the ventricles.<Cite n={1} /> It divides into the **right and left bundle branches**, which lead into a branching network of **Purkinje fibres** running through the walls of the ventricles.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The sinoatrial (SA) node is located in the superior and posterior walls of the right atrium.<Cite n={3} /> Its cells have the highest inherent rate of depolarisation in the heart, which is why it acts as the pacemaker.<Cite n={3} />

The impulse is carried from the SA node to the AV node by the internodal pathways (anterior, middle and posterior), although their importance is debated: the impulse would reach the AV node simply by passing cell to cell through the atrial myocardium. It reaches the left atrium through a separate interatrial band (Bachmann's bundle).<Cite n={3} /> The intercalated discs between myocardial cells contain many gap junctions that allow ions to pass between cells; this helps the cells contract in synchrony.<Cite n={3} />

The AV node lies within the triangle of Koch at the base of the right atrium.<Cite n={2} /> Histologically, it consists of small ovoid and spindle-shaped nodal myocytes embedded in a fibrous, collagen-rich matrix, with fewer gap junctions than working myocardium.<Cite n={2} /> In right-dominant circulation, the AV nodal artery most commonly arises from the right coronary artery.<Cite n={2} /> The compact AV node continues distally into the penetrating bundle of His.<Cite n={2} />

The bundle of His passes through the interventricular septum and divides into two bundle branches; the left bundle branch is considerably larger than the right and has two fascicles.<Cite n={3} /> Purkinje fibres conduct the impulse rapidly.<Cite n={3} /> Some fibres of the right bundle branch run in the moderator band to the papillary muscles of the right ventricle. As a result, they receive the impulse at approximately the same time and begin to contract just before the rest of the ventricular myocardium; this is believed to allow tension to develop on the chordae tendineae before right ventricular contraction.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## How it works

<Patient>

The sinus node sends out an impulse, which spreads across both atria: they contract and pump blood into the ventricles.<Cite n={3} /> The impulse then reaches the AV node, where it is briefly held up.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> This pause matters: it gives the atria time to finish contracting and fill the ventricles.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> Without the pause, the atria and ventricles would contract at the same time and blood would not flow properly through the heart.<Cite n={1} /> The AV node also limits how many very rapid impulses get through from the atria to the ventricles.<Cite n={2} />

After the pause, the impulse travels quickly along the bundle of His, the bundle branches and the Purkinje fibres, and the ventricles contract.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Contraction starts at the apex of the heart and moves towards the base, pushing blood out of the ventricles into the aorta and the pulmonary trunk.<Cite n={3} />

If the sinus node stops working, the AV node can take over setting the rhythm, but more slowly: about 40–60 beats per minute.<Cite n={[1, 2, 3]} />

**How this shows on an ECG.** The small P wave reflects excitation of the atria, the large QRS complex reflects excitation of the ventricles, and the T wave reflects their recovery (repolarisation).<Cite n={3} /> There is more about the waves in the [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/) article.

</Patient>

<Student>

The delay in the AV node is approximately 100 ms.<Cite n={3} /> It is partly due to the small diameter of the nodal cells and to conduction between nodal cells being less efficient than between conducting cells.<Cite n={3} /> This physiological conduction delay optimises ventricular filling and preserves atrioventricular synchrony, on which cardiac output and mechanical efficiency depend.<Cite n={2} />

The AV node also limits the transmission of excessively rapid atrial impulses to the ventricles.<Cite n={2} /> This is particularly important in supraventricular tachyarrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation.<Cite n={2} />

Pacemaker hierarchy: if a higher level fails, a lower one takes over, but at a lower inherent rate — the AV node at 40–60 per minute, the bundle of His at approximately 30–40, the bundle branches at 20–30 and the Purkinje fibres at 15–20.<Cite n={3} />

On the ECG, atrial repolarisation occurs during the QRS complex, which masks it.<Cite n={3} /> The PR interval starts at the beginning of the P wave and ends at the beginning of the QRS complex.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>
