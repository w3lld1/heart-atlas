The heart has four chambers: two upper chambers, the right and left atria, and two lower chambers, the right and left ventricles.<Cite n={1} /> The atria receive blood, and the ventricles are the heart's main pumps.<Cite n={3} /> The right atrium and right ventricle together are called the right heart, and the left atrium and left ventricle the left heart.<Cite n={1} /> The overall structure of the heart is described in the article [Structure of the heart](../heart-structure/); this article looks more closely at the chambers themselves.

  <p slot="systole">Ventricular systole. The ventricles contract and eject blood: the right into the pulmonary trunk, the left into the aorta. Meanwhile the atria are relaxed, and blood from the veins keeps flowing into them.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /></p>

## How it is built

<Patient>

The **<Structure id="right-atrium">right atrium</Structure>** receives oxygen-poor blood from the whole body except the lungs: it arrives through the superior and inferior venae cavae.<Cite n={[1, 2]} /> Blood from the heart muscle itself also drains here, through the coronary sinus.<Cite n={1} /> From the right atrium, blood passes through the tricuspid valve into the **<Structure id="right-ventricle">right ventricle</Structure>**.<Cite n={[2, 3]} /> The right ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary trunk, and from there it goes to the lungs.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

The **<Structure id="left-atrium">left atrium</Structure>** collects oxygen-rich blood returning from the lungs through the four pulmonary veins.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> Through the mitral valve it passes into the **<Structure id="left-ventricle">left ventricle</Structure>**.<Cite n={2} /> The left ventricle is the main pumping chamber of the left heart: it sends blood through the aortic valve into the aorta and on to the whole body.<Cite n={[1, 3]} />

Each atrium has an auricle, a small thin-walled pouch that can fill with blood; the name means "ear-like", because its shape resembles the outer ear.<Cite n={3} />

The wall of the left ventricle is much thicker than that of the right.<Cite n={3} /> The muscle layer of the heart wall, the myocardium, is thickest in the left ventricle.<Cite n={2} />

The chambers are separated by partitions called septa. The interatrial septum lies between the atria and the interventricular septum between the ventricles; the interventricular septum is substantially thicker than the interatrial one.<Cite n={3} /> The septum between the atria and the ventricles is called the atrioventricular septum.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

The interatrial septum bears an oval depression, the fossa ovalis, a remnant of the foramen ovale, which in the fetal heart allowed blood to pass directly from the right atrium to the left atrium.<Cite n={3} /> This opening closes soon after birth.<Cite n={[1, 3]} /> If the septum primum and septum secundum fail to fuse, the foramen ovale remains patent.<Cite n={2} /> The opening of the coronary sinus lies on the posterior surface of the right atrium, immediately superior and slightly medial to the opening of the inferior vena cava.<Cite n={3} />

The anterior surface of the right atrium and the right auricle have prominent ridges of muscle, the pectinate muscles (mm. pectinati); in the left atrium, pectinate muscles are found only in the auricle.<Cite n={3} /> The walls of both ventricles are lined with trabeculae carneae, ridges of cardiac muscle covered by endocardium.<Cite n={3} />

The flaps of the atrioventricular valves are attached by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles. There are three papillary muscles in the right ventricle and two in the left.<Cite n={3} /> The right ventricle has a moderator band, a band of cardiac muscle covered by endocardium; the left ventricle has none.<Cite n={3} /> The openings between the atria and ventricles are surrounded by rings of dense connective tissue, part of the fibrous skeleton of the heart.<Cite n={3} />

</Student>

## How it works

<Patient>

Contraction is called systole, and relaxation, during which the chambers fill with blood, is called diastole.<Cite n={4} /> The atria and ventricles do not contract at the same time: atrial systole ends before ventricular systole begins.<Cite n={4} />

Most blood flows passively into the ventricles while both the atria and the ventricles are relaxed.<Cite n={3} /> Towards the end of ventricular relaxation, the atria contract and push more blood into them: this accounts for about 20–30% of ventricular filling, and the remaining 70–80% flows in passively.<Cite n={[3, 4]} /> Then the ventricles contract and eject blood into the pulmonary trunk and the aorta, while the atria are already relaxed.<Cite n={[3, 4]} />

The right and left ventricles pump the same amount of blood per contraction.<Cite n={3} /> The left one needs a thicker wall because it pumps blood around the long systemic circulation and must generate high pressure; the pulmonary circuit is shorter and offers less resistance to blood flow.<Cite n={3} />

</Patient>

<Student>

Ventricular systole follows depolarisation of the ventricles and is represented by the QRS complex on the ECG; ventricular diastole follows repolarisation and is represented by the T wave.<Cite n={4} /> Atrial systole lasts approximately 100 ms.<Cite n={4} /> The phases are covered in more detail in the article [Cardiac cycle](../cardiac-cycle/), and the ECG in the article [ECG](../../diagnostic/ecg/).

When the myocardium of the ventricle contracts, pressure within the ventricular chamber rises.<Cite n={3} /> At the same time the papillary muscles contract, generating tension on the chordae tendineae, which prevents the flaps of the atrioventricular valves from being forced into the atria.<Cite n={3} /> The tricuspid valve lets blood into the right ventricle during diastole and closes during systole.<Cite n={2} /> Coordinated contraction of the ventricles begins at the apex and moves towards the base.<Cite n={2} /> How the valves are built and how they work is covered in the article [Heart valves](../heart-valves/).

</Student>
